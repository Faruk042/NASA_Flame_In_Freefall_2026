<p>Vehicle Modeling during the Burning of Thermally Thick 
</p>
<p>Samples in the Saffire V Experiments 
Justin Niehaus1, John Brooker2, David Urban3, Gary Ruff4 
</p>
<p>NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>In order to design a safer spacecraft in terms of fire prevention, detection and cleanup, the transport of 
</p>
<p>combustion products and heat must be predicted. Large-scale fire data was provided by the Saffire payload 
</p>
<p>within Northrup Grumman’s Cygnus vehicle after a resupply mission to the ISS. Solid materials, including 
</p>
<p>thick thermoplastic samples were ignited to study flame spread and the effect a fire has on a vehicle. Sensors 
</p>
<p>that measured species and temperature were placed within Cygnus. A model using Pyrosim was developed to 
</p>
<p>predict the thick fuel burn events from Saffire V by using sensor data from Cygnus. The heat transfer 
</p>
<p>coefficient between the cabin air and cargo was previously calibrated using the cotton fuel burn event, and 
</p>
<p>provided a good prediction of the temperature response within the vehicle during the two thick fuel 
</p>
<p>experiments. The difference between the Saffire inlet CO2 sensor, and the forward port standoff CO2 sensor, 
</p>
<p>which is the closest to the Saffire outlet, was used as an input into the model. This method provided an excellent 
</p>
<p>prediction of the CO2 response in the other parts of the Cygnus. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>spacecraft fire poses one of the greatest risks to the crew and mission success. There is a need to predict the 
</p>
<p>consequences of a fire event to reduce the risk for long-duration missions. Most flame spread testing has been 
</p>
<p>executed in 1-g or has not been large-scale1,2,3. The Saffire project has provided valuable data on large flame spread 
</p>
<p>in a microgravity environment while acting as a test bed for novel gas species sensor technology3,4. These series of 
</p>
<p>experiments have taken place during the end of a resupply mission to the to the International Space Station (ISS) on 
</p>
<p>Northrop Grumman’s (NG) Cygnus vehicle. After Cygnus undocks from the ISS, the experiments take place and the 
</p>
<p>data is downlinked before destructive re-entry into the earth’s atmosphere. The data collected will help designers and 
</p>
<p>modelers predict the effects of a large-scale fire in a microgravity environment in order to design a safer spacecraft. 
</p>
<p> The focus of the first three Saffire missions was on flame spread5,6,7,8, while the Saffire IV and V expanded the 
</p>
<p>experiment to include gas release events and sensors throughout the vehicle. These sensors include diagnostic tools to 
</p>
<p>determine the temperature increase due to the fire and transport of combustion products such as CO2. The data 
</p>
<p>collected from these far-field gas and particle sensors will provide insight into smoke plume and combustion product 
</p>
<p>transport within the vehicle9,10,11. The test events include flow visualization at the beginning and end of the campaign, 
</p>
<p>CO2 scrubs, gas releases, and burn events. Both Saffire IV and V experiments utilized six remote sensors (RS) spread 
</p>
<p>throughout Cygnus that measure temperature and product concentration, such as CO2, as well as a Far Field Device 
</p>
<p>(FFD) that is representative of average temperature and species concentration in the cabin. Both Saffire experiments 
</p>
<p>had cotton based and Polymethyl (Methacrylate) (PMMA) samples as fuel for the burn events. 
</p>
<p>The focus of this paper is on the two PMMA burn events in Saffire V. Similar to previous work12,13, the effect of 
</p>
<p>burning the PMMA samples on the vehicle temperature and combustion product spread are predicted. The geometry 
</p>
<p>and cargo configuration for Saffire V is the same that was used in previous work that explored the cotton sample in 
</p>
<p>Saffire V13. The effective convective heat transfer between the cargo and the cabin air that was calibrated in that same 
</p>
<p>work is also used in this work, which was calibrated to fit the closest RS to the Saffire outlet. The development of this 
</p>
<p>model will aid in the prediction of heat and mass transfer throughout a spacecraft after a fire. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>II. Saffire Experiment 
</p>
<p>Saffire I – V have all taken place on the NG Cygnus vehicle. The experimental data collection and downlinking 
</p>
<p>happened after the cargo from the resupply mission was unloaded onto the ISS and the trash was packed into Cygnus. 
</p>
<p>A schematic of a nearly empty Cygnus vehicle is provided in Figure 1 before the cargo is loaded, highlighting the 
</p>
<p>Saffire payload and instruments used to collect data. The pressure vessel is approximately 4 meters in length and 3 
</p>
<p>meters in diameter. The Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) recirculated flow throughout the 
</p>
<p>vehicle by taking air from the zenith side, near the (FFD), and expelling it on the nadir side where Saffire is located. 
</p>

<p>Trash and cargo were placed by the crew in a manner which constructed interconnected channels of approximately 
</p>
<p>triangular shape form, as shown in Figure 2. These channels, referred to as standoffs, transported species, smoke, and 
</p>
<p>heat from the outlet of the Saffire payload during a gas release or burn event. The heat addition rate (HAR) and the 
</p>
<p>mass generation rate (MGR), which supply the heat and combustion products from the Saffire payload to the Cygnus 
</p>
<p>cabin, are applied to the Saffire outlet, which has a surface area of 50 cm2. The flow of gas generally went towards 
</p>
<p>the zenith section of Cygnus from the Saffire outlet through the forward port (FP) standoff. From there, heat and 
</p>
<p>species would either diffuse towards the other three standoffs, or flow back to the nadir section through the ECLSS 
</p>
<p>system. 
</p>

<p>RS is positioned, to the zenith section where RS6 and the FFD are located. B) This view shows the aft side of the 
</p>

<p>Table 1 presents the experimental events completed in the Saffire V campaign. Similar to Saffire IV, Saffire V 
</p>
<p>started with a CO2 scrub by pulling cabin air through the FFD. Event 2 released CO2 in the cabin to help determine 
</p>
<p>the free volume used to calculate the amount of oxygen that needed to be added between burns. In addition to CO2, 
</p>
<p>HF was released to study its decay in the atmosphere since it tends to adsorb to surfaces more than other combustion 
</p>
<p>products such as CO2. A flow visualization test was performed to determine any major differences in the Saffire Flow 
</p>
<p>Unit (SFU) after all the planned burns were completed in event 3 and 9. Event 6 burned a one-sided PMMA, while 
</p>
<p>event 8 burned a thicker structured PMMA with grooves and ridges of increasing dimension or separation9. The 
</p>
<p>atmosphere for the experimental campaign was approximately 0.71 atm and 29% oxygen, with an initial temperature 
</p>
<p>of approximately 24.1°C. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>III. Modeling 
</p>
<p>By using Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS), a code developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology 
</p>
<p>(NIST), a model was constructed that predicted the effect of the PMMA burn events from Saffire V on the Cygnus 
</p>
<p>Vehicle. The focus of this large-eddy turbulent simulation code is the low-speed flow created by a compartment fire 
</p>
<p>as well as smoke and heat transfer14. PyrosimTM 15 is the graphical interface that was used to run the FDS simulations 
</p>
<p>and develop the vehicle model. A picture of this model highlighting the FFD, SFU, ECLSS system and RS locations 
</p>
<p>is provided by Figure 2. A cargo placement map and photographs provided by NG were used to construct the geometry 
</p>
<p>in Pyrosim, as was discussed in a previous paper discussing the cotton burning sample (event 5)13. </p>
<p></p>

<p>The six remote sensors served as the main comparisons between the model and experiments. While all remote 
</p>
<p>sensors are visible in Figure 1 without the cargo, some of the remote sensors are not visible due to the stowed material 
</p>
<p>in Figure 2. Starting with the nadir section of Cygnus where Saffire is located, RS1 is positioned in the bulkhead. RS 
</p>
<p>2 -5 are in the standoffs at the midplane (zenith to nadir). Starting at the forward starboard (FS) where RS2 is located, 
</p>
<p>the others are numbered by moving counterclockwise about the other standoffs: RS3 - forward port (FP), RS4 - aft 
</p>
<p>port (AP), and RS5 - aft starboard (AS). RS6 is in the zenith near the hatch. In both burn events, the initial RS reading 
</p>
<p>for the CO2 was within 5% of the average, and hence this average was used for the initial CO2 in the simulation of 
</p>
<p>each event. The initial temperature reading of the RS varied more than this in each event, hence the initial reading of 
</p>
<p>RS3 was used as the initial temperature of the cabin since that is the RS that created the greatest response. The 
</p>
<p>simulation curves of the other remote sensors were then adjusted so the initial values matched. 
</p>
<p> Table 2 gives the flowrates used within Cygnus and Saffire. The flow rate of 0.028 m3/s utilized by the SFU gave 
</p>
<p>a flow of 20 cm/s over the samples, which is ramped down at certain times to extinguish the flame. In order to capture 
</p>
<p>the effect of the burn event on the temperature of Cygnus, the HAR was calculated using the inlet and outlet 
</p>
<p>temperature of the SFU, as given by Eq. 1, and demonstrated in Figure 3 using data from event 8. This method was 
</p>
<p>utilized in previous work12,13, and is described as the sensible enthalpy rise method highlighted by the Society of Fire 
</p>
<p>Protection Engineering Handbook16. No combustion or flame properties are modeled, hence the Saffire payload is 
</p>
<p>modeled simply as an object that takes in cool cabin air and outputs hot air and combustion products. Any radiation 
</p>
<p>from the Saffire unit or the hot exhaust gases has been assumed to be negligible. Other properties of the initial 
</p>
<p>atmospheric conditions of the Cygnus vehicle are provided by Table 3. The properties of air at initial conditions (24°C) 
</p>
<p>are used to calculate the HAR since the temperature at the outlet does not exceed 45°C, and the difference in the main 
</p>
<p>combustion products (CO2 and H2O) are only in the 1000s of parts per million (ppm), making a negligible difference 
</p>
<p>in cp and density between SFU inlet and outlet conditions. 
</p>

</p>
<p>was not realized in the PMMA burn events. In order to determine the combustion products that entered into the cabin 
</p>
<p>from the Saffire payload, the data from the experimental sensors were used. RS3 acted as the outlet sensor, since the </p>

</p>

<p>IV. Results and Discussion 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> The initial experimental CO2 values for burn event 6 ranged from 7,375 to 7,973 ppm, giving an average of 7,597 
</p>
<p>ppm as given by Table 3. This average value was used for the initial value for the event 6 simulation, and then the 
</p>
<p>prediction curves were increased or decreased to match the initial experimental values. Figure 7 shows the model 
</p>
<p>prediction with the experimental results for the CO2 response of the six remote sensors. An excellent agreement is 
</p>
<p>realized in each case. There is slight over prediction during the increasing part of the curve in RS3 and a moderate 
</p>
<p>overprediction of this part of the curve in RS6, which are in the direct path of the flow from Saffire from the FP 
</p>
<p>standoff. While the model has solid trash and cargo lining the edges of the FP standoff, these solids are porous in 
</p>
<p>reality, allowing for more mixing of air and combustion products on the way to RS3 and RS6, which would bring the 
</p>
<p>peak of the combustion product response down. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 7. Saffire V RS CO2 responses during one-sided PMMA burn test (event 6). 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 8 provides the temperature prediction during the one-sided PMMA burn. A convective heat transfer 
</p>
<p>coefficient of 150 W/m2-K was used for the surfaces, calibrated at RS3 from the cotton burn in the previous work13. 
</p>
<p>The model initial condition used the RS3 initial condition (24.1 °C) since that was the RS that had the largest response. </p>

<p>However, the remote sensors ranged from 19.2 to 24.3 °C initially. Since the initial temperatures of the remote sensors 
</p>
<p>were all different, the prediction curves were adjusted so that the initial value matched that of each location, in order 
</p>
<p>to compare the increase in temperature. There was very little response besides RS3, which increased approximately 1 
</p>
<p>degree at the peak and reached steady state at about a half degree increase. There was negligible temperature difference 
</p>
<p>in the starboard standoffs (RS2 and RS5) compared to the sensitivity of the thermocouples (+/- 0.25°C). This was 
</p>
<p>predicted well by the model. 
</p>
<p> Figure 9 presents the RS predictions for the structured PMMA. Excellent agreement is realized again for all RS 
</p>
<p>values, especially at steady state where both prediction and experiment show about a 2,500 ppm increase or a 36% 
</p>
<p>increase in CO2 throughout the cabin. The model did predict a larger maximum value of CO2 for both RS3 and RS6 
</p>
<p>which are in the direct flow path of the Saffire combustion products. It is possible that the bags of trash, which are not 
</p>
<p>perfectly rigid, partially blocked those sensors, making the CO2 have to diffuse around the bag to get to the sensor, 
</p>
<p>allowing for a smoother response. Fluctuations in both the CO2 and temperature response of the model can be observed 
</p>
<p>by the remote sensors within the flow path and are placed in a more open part of the cabin outside of the standoffs, 
</p>
<p>such as in RS6. This is likely due to the turbulent nature of the model. The reason these fluctuations are not as 
</p>
<p>pronounced in the data is the remote sensors use a pump to suck combustion products the location to the actual sensor. 
</p>
<p>Even if it is a small area, it is averaging the concentration around the probe area, likely smoothing out the effect of 
</p>
<p>turbulence. </p>

<p> The predictions for RS temperature response during the structured PMMA burn are provided in Figure 10. The 
</p>
<p>Saffire V temperatures during the structured PMMA burn, similar to the one-sided PMMA burn case, provide very 
</p>
<p>little response outside of RS3 and RS6, which is predicted by the model. Integrating the heat addition rate from Figures 
</p>
<p>4 and 5 provide a total energy addition of 233 and 275 kJ for event 6 and event 8 respectively. Despite this moderate 
</p>
<p>difference in total heat added, the temperature response remains nearly the same for both burn events. The results from 
</p>
<p>the experiments and model, with regards to the specific spacecraft and test conditions, indicate that the fire does not 
</p>
<p>have a large effect on the vehicle temperature, except close to the fire. 
</p>
<p> An interesting phenomenon is observed by looking at the temperature profile in RS4 for both burn cases. The 
</p>
<p>experimental profile decreases at some point below the initial temperature. One theory is the ECLSS system on Cygnus 
</p>
<p>is known to cycle on an off to keep the conditions of the spacecraft within certain parameters. RS4 is in the direct path 
</p>
<p>of the ECLSS system, and on the opposite side of the hot Saffire outlet, which is why it makes sense its profile is 
</p>
<p>dictated more by the ECLSS system as opposed to the fire. RS1, which is even closer to the ECLSS outlet, shows a 
</p>
<p>less pronounced decrease in temperature, but since it is close to the Saffire outlet, its behavior is more dictated by the 
</p>
<p>burn event. </p>

<p>V. Conclusions 
</p>
<p> A FDS code was developed to determine the effects of the Saffire V PMMA burn events on the Cygnus vehicle. 
</p>
<p>Measurements of CO2 and temperature were taken throughout the vehicle, specifically at six RS locations. The 
</p>
<p>combustion product generation rates were calculated based on the scaled difference of RS3 and the SFU inlet CO2 
</p>
<p>readings and the stoichiometry of PMMA combustion, while the heat addition rate was calculated based on the inlet 
</p>
<p>and outlet Saffire temperatures. Parts of the model cargo were hollowed out in order for the model volume to match 
</p>
<p>the volume calculated from the CO2 release events. The convective heat transfer coefficient that was calibrated at RS3 
</p>
<p>for the Saffire V cotton burn in previous work was determined to be 150 W/m2-K and gave and excellent prediction 
</p>
<p>for both Saffire V PMMA burn events. The model did an excellent job of predicting the CO2 and a reasonable job of 
</p>
<p>predicting temperature data. The effect a spacecraft fire has on a vehicle was highlighted by the Saffire data and model, 
</p>
<p>such as product mixing and heat transfer. These results demonstrate the feasibility of predicting the impact of a fire 
</p>
<p>on the habitability of an orbiting spacecraft. 
</p>
