Hydrogen chloride (HCl) is a major combustion product from the pyrolysis of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) 

insulated electrical wires, a common spacecraft fire safety concern. Models at two different scales were 

developed to predict HCl uptake on anodized, chromate conversion coated (Iridite), and bare aluminum 

surfaces, as well as on Nomex fabric: a macroscopic one-step global surface reaction model where all the active 

sites are on the exterior surface, and a pore model where the interior active sites deeper into the oxide layer 

can also be accessed by HCl. Experiments were performed to calibrate kinetic and diffusion constants in the 

models. A cast acrylic test cell was used to measure the differences between the inlet and outlet concentration 

of HCl after inserting a sample rod of the test material. For the materials with a thin (< 200 µm) or no oxide 

layer, the macroscopic surface reaction model adequately predicts the experimental measurements. For the 

anodized aluminum with a thicker oxide layer, the pore model provided a better match to experimental results. 

The results will be discussed with respect to the spacecraft fire safety project (Saffire).

I. Introduction 

Electrical fires are a common spacecraft safety concern. Acid gases, such as hydrogen chloride (HCl), hydrogen 

fluoride (HF) and hydrogen cyanide (HCN) are combustion products from the pyrolysis of electrical wire insulation 

typically found inside a spacecraft. It is important to track acid gases for potential fire detection applications as well 

as general crew health and safety concerns. Unlike other combustion products such as CO2 and H2O, acid gas does 

not stay in the atmosphere long because it rapidly interacts with surfaces1,2. Knowing the capacity and rate of acid gas 

uptake of surfaces will determine how much stays in the atmosphere for detection purposes, as well as determining 

when the cabin becomes safe for habitation after a fire. Determining the uptake by the surfaces and the products 

contained within the surface will help with the post fire cleanup effort. 

 Aluminum is a metal abundantly found inside a spacecraft, due to its strong and lightweight nature. Like most 

metals, aluminum has a natural oxide layer that protects it from corrosion. This oxide layer is often enhanced with 

surface treatments such as chromate conversion (Iridite) and anodization3,4,5,6. The oxide layer, referred to as aluminum 

oxide or alumina is affected by the water vapor in the atmosphere, changing its structure and reactivity7,8. Alumina 

has been shown to uptake HCl from the atmosphere9,10,11. 

Another common material found inside a spacecraft is Nomex, a flame-resistant synthetic polymer related to nylon. 

This commercially available material is used for cargo storage bags and as a fire barrier. It is particularly abundant in 

cargo vehicles like Cygnus where Nomex bags are used to contain trash and other items to be removed from the 

International Space Station (ISS). This material has also been shown to uptake acid gases12. 

 Previous work has shown that HCl uptake on anodized aluminum is significantly more than an Iridite treated or 

stock aluminum with a natural oxide layer9,10. While the oxide layer is significantly thicker for anodized aluminum, 

the structure is also unique. Anodized aluminum form reproducible circular pores, often in a hexagonal array13,14,15. 

This adds additional surface area, and the HCl can diffuse down into the pores. Hence, a modeling approach which 

accounts for these factors is expected to provide more accurate uptake predictions. 

 This work builds upon earlier studies in predicting the interaction of HCl with materials in the context of a 

spacecraft fire9,10,16. A two-dimensional (2D) axisymmetric model of the test cell was previously developed to capture 

the interaction of HCl with aluminum and was expanded to predict HCl uptake by Nomex. This model, referred to as 

the “surface model”, features a one-step global surface kinetic rate with parameters that were calibrated from a baseline 

experimental case. A second model was developed to capture the physics taking place in the scale of the oxide layer. 

This model captured the diffusion of HCl into the open pores inside the oxide layer of the anodized aluminum surface, 

The analysis in this work shows that the surface model can predict the uptake of HCl by Nomex and the thin oxide 

layer aluminum surfaces (stock and Iridite); however, for anodized aluminum, the oxide layer needs to be modeled to 

predict the uptake of HCl and to determine the depth chlorine diffuses into the oxide layer.

II. Experimental Methods 

An apparatus was constructed that was used in previous studies to determine the uptake of HCl by surfaces of 

interest9,10,16. Hard samples such as aluminum were fashioned into circular rods of 2.54 cm diameter to be inserted 

into the test cell after it had been saturated with HCl. For fabrics, the sample was wrapped around an acrylic rod that 

was inserted into the test cell after it had been saturated. Figure 1 shows the experimental setup. 

The test cell is made of cast acrylic due to its low reactivity with acid gases. For this experimental series, HCl was 

injected from a source bottle with a concentration of approximately 50 ppm in air. The air used for dilution is zero 

ultra-high purity. Mass flow controllers (MFC) allowed for the flow of HCl and diluting air up to 5000 cubic 

centimeters per minute (ccm) with an accuracy of 50 ccm. HCl was measured with an accuracy of 0.1 ppm at the inlet 

of the test cell to confirm the gas mixture, and at the outlet. These two measurements were used to determine the 

amount of HCl adsorbed on the surface. 

A typical experiment would start by making HCl flow into the empty test cell until adsorption onto the walls of 

the test cell reached equilibrium. This was deemed to have been achieved when the inlet and outlet sensors reached 

equal values. A total flow of 1000 ccm was used in all tests. After saturation, the sample was inserted into the test cell 

and the HCl sensor responses were recorded. 

The 6061-T6 aluminum rods used in this study were anodized by MIL-A-8625F Type II Class 1, were treated by 

chrome conversion (Iridite) using MIL-DTL-5541F Type I, or left untreated. The Nomex used was HT90-40 (Stern 

and Stern Industries Inc., Hornell, NY, USA). 

III. Modeling 

Two types of models were considered: a surface model and a pore model. The surface model is a model of the test 

cell and was developed to calibrate surface kinetic parameters of a one-step global surface reaction. The predictions 

from this model proved accurate for thin (under 200 nm) oxide layer surfaces and Nomex but were less suited for 

thicker oxide layer surfaces. Therefore, the pore model was adopted, which accounts for the diffusion of HCl into the 

pores of the anodized aluminum oxide layer, and the reaction of that HCl with the pore walls. Figure 2 shows a 

schematic of the model with the boundary conditions.

B. Pore Model 

 X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses of samples revealed that chlorine diffuses deeper into the 

anodized oxide layer. This explains why the anodized aluminum has a higher uptake capacity and is possibly the 

reason for why the surface model is inadequate to predict the uptake of anodized samples. Since chlorine is found 

deeper inside the anodized surface, it stands to reason that active surface sites are also deeper down in the oxide layer, 

requiring a multiscale model that can account for acid gas diffusion through the oxide layer. 

 As previously mentioned, anodized aluminum has been modeled as hexagonal cells of pores. McIntosh et al. 

studied the adsorption of hydrogen fluoride (HF) in smelter grade alumina, indicating that clogging of the pores is the 

mechanism of saturation, while determining a minimum pore diameter in which uptake of HF becomes ineffective19,20. 

When HCl reaches the alumina at the pore walls, it creates a deposit of aluminum chloride or aluminum 

hydrochloride,21,22. This deposit can be porous, and HCl must diffuse through it to get to the wall for further reaction, 

creating more deposition and resistance to reach the reaction front buried even deeper, similar to the shrinking core 

model used in coal combustion23,24.

particles hitting the wall is often accounted for by a decrease in the diffusion coefficient. The diffusion of gaseous 

HCl through air is well known. Gaseous diffusion in 

general is on the order of 10-5 m2/s, or in the case of HCl 

through air at room temperature, 1.55x10-5 m2/s30,31.

assumes the uptake is happening in an atmosphere void of water vapor, even though the nominal humidity level on 

the ISS is between 40 and 60% humidity. This is before considering the water vapor released into the atmosphere from

gets desorbed from previous events at higher temperatures. Ground testing in the future can be used to verify these 

results, as well as to calibrate temperature dependent terms in the models. 

V. Conclusions 

Determining the fate of acid gases after a fire in spacecraft is an important part of crew health and safety. These 

results lay the foundation to predict the fate of acid gas during a spacecraft fire. The uptake of HCl by common surfaces 

found inside a spacecraft were predicted using two models at different scales. For surfaces with a thin or no oxide 

layer, a macroscopic surface model where the saturation of the sample is due to filling of a thin layer of active surface 

sites, adequately predicts experimental results. For metal oxides with a thick oxide layer, a pore model that accounts 

for the diffusion of HCl into the pore before it reaches the active surface sites can predict the results better than the 

surface model wherein the effect of the oxide layer is neglected. The pore model has the added advantage of predicting 

the depth chlorine can be found in the oxide layer.
