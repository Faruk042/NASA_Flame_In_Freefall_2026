<div class="slide-content"><p>Why vehicle and suit atmospheres matter</p>
<p>Spacesuit operating pressure is set as low as possible to maximize mobility and minimize fatigue during a spacewalk</p>
<p>Conversely, vehicles operate at a higher pressure than suits to mitigate other issues (More on this later)</p>
<p>Like SCUBA diving, changing pressures on the human body comes with risk of Decompression Sickness (DCS - a release of nitrogen dissolved in tissues which may result in bubble formation)</p>
<p>Venous Gas Emboli (VGE) are “silent bubbles” (no symptoms or clinical findings)</p>
<p>Type I DCS = mild symptoms: joint pain; single extremity tingling or numbness</p>
<p>Type II DCS = can be life-threatening: Central neurological or cardiopulmonary symptoms</p>
<p>Independent Variables considered include prebreathe duration, suit pressure, and vehicle atmosphere, and dependent Variables include crew timeline and crew fatigue. DCS and Flammability risks are meant to
be held constant.</p>
<p>An Exploration Atmosphere “Issue Resolution Team” (IRT) was set up in ~Dec 2022 to directly work the problem of lunar surface mission atmospheres</p>
<p>The ExAtm IRT consisted of technical and programmatic experts from across the Agency</p>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Implementation considerations</p>
<p>Higher suit pressure: perform testing in flight-analog suits at higher pressure to determine any impacts to crew fatigue, mobility, ability to perform EVA tasks</p>
<p></p>
<p>DCS risk: perform testing (e.g. JSC 20' chamber) to gather data on anatomical impacts from various prebreathe protocols given a starting vehicle atmosphere</p>
<p></p>
<p>Lower vehicle pressure: work to understand/quantify flammability risk at lower pressure (and 1/6g) from probability of ignition to material flammability to ability to detect and suppress a fire</p>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Early mission architectures and future work</p>
<p>The ExAtm IRT was charged with making a decision about early lunar surface mission atmospheres 
in Summer 2024</p>
<p>Extensive test programs had started producing data, but unknowns/uncertainty drove a recommendation for 
vehicle atmospheres to stay below a 30% O2 maximum for “early” missions</p>
<p>Example: material flammability at O2 above 30% was extremely limited and showed “worse” behavior</p>
<p>Example: testing at various O2%’s resulted in “propagation where we didn’t expect and no propagation where we expected it”</p>
<p>SME work had shown that NASA’s approach to fire safety since Apollo/Skylab was oversimplified and inadequately considered complexity in how fire starts, spreads, and is suppressed</p>
<p>Example: exposing the face of a material to fire may not cause propagation but exposing the edge might</p>
<p>Example: adding zippers or thread to a material may drastically change how it responds to fire</p>
<p>30% O2 limit for early missions drove urgency on Elevated Suit Pressure (ESP) testing/analysis and on optimizing prebreathe protocols to preserve as much EVA time as possible</p>
<p>While American spacesuits historically operate at 4.3psi, ESP testing/analysis is ongoing at 5.2psi</p>
<p>Eight 20’ chamber Prebreathe testing campaigns have occurred so far with more planned</p>
<p></p>
<p>ESP, Prebreathe, and Fire Safety testing/analysis is ongoing to inform future missions/vehicles</p>
<p>Long term desire remains to safely operate vehicles at elevated O2 to maximize useful EVA time</p>
<p>Will continue to work with Artemis commercial partners (Axiom, SpaceX, Blue Origin) to solve this problem</p>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Vehicle/suit atmosphere balancing involves many conflicting variables and passionate stakeholders</p>
<p>Fire in space is scary for a reason (Apollo 1, etc.)</p>
<p></p>
<p>Past assumptions or NASA standards about fire safety may no longer be valid</p>
<p>Simple/easy/cheap approaches/analyses from Shuttle/ISS era should not apply to Artemis</p>
<p></p>
<p>Small changes to powered hardware and/or material configuration can have unexpected impacts to ignition and fire behavior</p>
<p>Flight-like, configuration-specific testing is needed to quantify responses</p>
<p></p>
<p>Early Artemis missions may have operational limitations (longer prebreathe, suit mobility,
strict stowage rules) as we continue to explore this problem</p>
<p>2028 Artemis missions will be here soon!</p>
