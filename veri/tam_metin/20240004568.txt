<p>♦ Rotating Detonation Rocket Engines (RDREs) may yield system 
mass savings &amp; performance gains at the vehicle level.
 Higher performance enabled by pressure-gain combustion
 Vehicle trade studies were performed to evaluate the gains and losses 
</p>
<p>enacted by using an RDRE
</p>
<p>♦ Vehicle trade studies presented in this work are intended to be the 
first of many
 Intent is to understand how RDREs fit into system design for a wide variety of 
</p>
<p>vehicles
 Highlight use-cases that are worth pursuing
 Highlight vehicle-level impacts of RDRE, good and bad
</p>
<p>♦ Study #1: Liquid Mars Ascent Vehicle (L-MAV)
 NASA design, not flown. In-space, pressure-fed, hypergolic
 Single use and mass constrained: provides opportunity to evaluate RDRE 
</p>
<p>impact on system mass
</p>
<p>♦ Study #2: MIURA-1 Sounding Rocket
 Developed by Payload Aerospace S.K. (PLD Space), Flown once (Oct 2023). 
</p>
<p>Kerolox, pressure-fed
 Regeneratively cooled and reusable: provides opportunity to evaluate 
</p>
<p>regenerative cooling impacts for RDREs

<p>Workflow for L-MAV Trade Study
</p>
<p>♦ The independent variables for the L-MAV trade 
study are chamber pressure and throat area
 Performance codes are used to calculate engine 
</p>
<p>performance – namely thrust and specific impulse
 Codes account for heat losses to walls, non-ideal 
</p>
<p>combustion efficiency
 RDRE performance code developed in-house at GRC, 
</p>
<p>available here: https://software.nasa.gov/software/LEW-
20423-1
</p>
<p>♦ Mass estimation models calculate GLOM required to 
meet mission requirements
 Propellant tank and pressurant masses calculated as 
</p>
<p>function of required propellant tank pressure and volume
 Delta-V required calculated as function of thrust-to-weight 
</p>
<p>ratios for each stage
 Iterative solver converges on design parameters
 Component masses for avionics, power, feed system,etc. 
</p>
<p>are held constant at baseline values
 Further details in paper
</p>
<p>♦ Parametric sweep optimizes the design for minimum 
GLOM

<p>L-MAV Study Conclusions
</p>
<p>♦ L-MAV study highlights desirable characteristics of 
RDRES
 Pressure-gain combustion enables higher mass flow for a given 
</p>
<p>throat area and tank pressure
 Can reduce propellant tank pressure (to reduce weight) and 
</p>
<p>accommodate a larger nozzle area ratio (to increase ISP)
 Compounding effects – “tyranny of the rocket equation” result in 
</p>
<p>large mass savings
</p>

<p>Regenerative Cooling Model
</p>
<p>♦ MIURA-1 assessment must include a regenerative 
cooling model
 The regenerative cooling jacket is modeled with 1-D, steady flow
 Channel dimensions are allowed to vary along flow path to 
</p>
<p>minimize pressure drop while respecting user-input geometry 
limits and temperature limits
</p>
<p> Axially-varying temperature, pressure, and fluid properties are 
calculated for exhaust and coolant
</p>
<p> Bartz and Sieder-Tate correlations used
</p>
<p>♦ Regenerative cooling model is used to calculate:
 Jacket ΔP, Jacket ΔT
 Performance loss due to exhaust heat losses
 Engine mass
</p>
<p>♦ Regenerative cooling is a sensitive problem
 We validated our model using RL-10 test data, with excellent 
</p>
<p>agreement for Jacket ΔP, Jacket ΔT, and performance loss. 
However, RL-10 is a conventional rocket engine.
</p>
<p> Sparse experimental data for RDRE heat transfer, so there is 
uncertainty in the RDRE heat transfer calculation
</p>
<p>MIURA-1 Sounding Rocket RDRE Trade Study (2/6)
</p>
<p>Heat transfer model validation 
data is available in paper.</p>

<p>Regenerative Cooling Analysis
</p>
<p>♦ RDRE chamber pressure is described using term “initial 
pressure”
 This is the manifold pressure minus the injector pressure drop. It is 
</p>
<p>the pressure that supplies the RDRE chamber.
</p>
<p>♦ Engine thrust was held constant at 6,800 lbf
 Initial pressure swept across 160 psia – 320 psia
 For all cases, chamber diameter is set such that thrust is 6,800 lbf
</p>
<p>♦ Predicted RDRE wall heat loads are very high
 In conventional combustion chambers, peak heat flux typically 
</p>
<p>occurs where flow is near-sonic (i.e. near throat)
 In RDRE combustion chamber, entire flow field is near sonic, and 
</p>
<p>there is more surface area available due to detonation channel 
geometry
</p>
<p>♦ Uncertainty in required chamber length, so maximum 
possible chamber length (respecting kerosene coking 
limit) was used for three cases:
 Kerosene cooling only
 Dual commodity cooling: Kerosene cooled inner body, liquid oxygen 
</p>
<p>cooled outer body
 Dual commodity cooling with mixture ratio reduced to 1.90
</p>
<p>MIURA-1 Sounding Rocket RDRE Trade Study (3/6)
</p>
<p>Predicted wall heat flux for one RDRE 
configuration, compared to MIURA-1’s 
conventional rocket engine (TEPREL-B) </p>

<p>Conclusion
</p>
<p>♦ L-MAV trade study looked at an in-space RDRE application
 RDRE powered vehicle predicted to have 8% lower mass than conventionally powered vehicle
 Suggests there is a use case for small, in-space RDREs in 300 to 1,500 lbf thrust class
 Primary benefit identified: reduced propellant tank pressure &amp; throat area for a given thrust level.
</p>
<p>♦ MIURA-1 trade study looked at a boost-phase regeneratively cooled RDRE application
 RDRE regenerative cooling jackets predicted to have high heat loads and high pressure drops 
</p>
<p>compared to conventional engine, requiring higher propellant tank pressures
 Even with impacts of regenerative cooling challenges, RDRE powered sounding rocket predicted to 
</p>
<p>achieve 12% more microgravity time
 For a reference mission, RDRE powered sounding rocket can be 8% shorter and 11% lighter
 Suggests there is a use case for boost-phase RDREs in 6,800 lbf thrust class
 Primary benefit identified: increased specific impulse from pressure-gain combustion
</p>
