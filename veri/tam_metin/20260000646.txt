<p>MATERIAL FLAMMABILITY AT LUNAR AND MARTIAN GRAVITY. Paul Ferkul,1 Nathan Kralik2, and 
</p>
<p>Ya-Ting Liao2 
</p>
<p>1Universities Space Research Association, NASA Glenn Research Center, 21000 Brookpark Rd., Cleveland, OH 
</p>
<p>44135, paul.ferkul@nasa.gov) 2Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Introduction: NASA relies on NASA-STD-6001B 
</p>
<p>Test 1 for screening material flammability for flight 
</p>
<p>[1]. The test is conducted in normal Earth gravity, 
</p>
<p>with the assumption that if a material passes the 1-g 
</p>
<p>test, then it is considered safe for spaceflight. This 
</p>
<p>approach has provided a practical means to assess the 
</p>
<p>great number of materials that are used currently, and 
</p>
<p>which have been used in the past. But the approach 
</p>
<p>has important uncertainties. Microgravity research 
</p>
<p>over the last few decades has shown that normal 
</p>
<p>gravity may not necessarily represent the most 
</p>
<p>flammable condition for a material. Theoretical and 
</p>
<p>experimental results have demonstrated that some 
</p>
<p>materials that will not burn in 1-g will burn in 0-g 
</p>
<p>(with a low-speed flow) or in partial gravity (such as 
</p>
<p>Lunar-g). 
</p>
<p>This can be understood by examining the role of 
</p>
<p>buoyant flow on flame spread. On Earth, buoyancy 
</p>
<p>causes hot product gases to accelerate upwards and out 
</p>
<p>of the flame, at the same time drawing fresh oxidizer 
</p>
<p>into the flame base. This introduction of fresh oxygen 
</p>
<p>sustains the flame. The speed of the air entering the 
</p>
<p>flame can be so high, however, that the chemical 
</p>
<p>reactions are unable to keep pace. Furthermore, the 
</p>
<p>rate of heat release and/or transport may be too low to 
</p>
<p>provide the energy to heat the incoming cold flow. 
</p>
<p>These effects can lead to flame de-stabilization, 
</p>
<p>commonly known as blowoff. The governing non-
</p>
<p>dimensional parameter is the Damkohler Number, 
</p>
<p>which is the ratio of the characteristic flow time to the 
</p>
<p>chemical reaction time. 
</p>
<p>Consider a material that is marginally non-
</p>
<p>flammable on Earth. The same material may be 
</p>
<p>flammable at a lower gravity level (for example, on the 
</p>
<p>Moon) since the crucial mechanism of buoyant oxygen 
</p>
<p>entrainment at the flame base is still present, but at a 
</p>
<p>slower rate, allowing the chemical reaction rates to 
</p>
<p>“keep up” and the flame to adequately heat the 
</p>
<p>incoming cool air. Alternatively, a material that is not 
</p>
<p>flammable on Earth can be burned in microgravity, but 
</p>
<p>with the introduction of a low speed forced flow of air. 
</p>
<p>Applying a flow below the buoyant blowoff limit 
</p>
<p>speed permits a material to burn in microgravity, albeit 
</p>
<p>with a smaller flame size, even though it may not burn 
</p>
<p>at all in 1-g. 
</p>
<p>Current Knowledge: Using drop towers, aircraft, 
</p>
<p>sounding rockets, and modeling, it is observed that 
</p>
<p>certain partial-g environments are expected to increase 
</p>
<p>the flammability limits for some materials. Early 
</p>
<p>numerical and experimental evidence suggested that 
</p>
<p>Lunar gravity could be more hazardous, since flame 
</p>
<p>spread rate as a function of gravity peaks there [2]. 
</p>
<p>Related work suggests that oxygen concentration for 
</p>
<p>flammability reaches a minimum value in the vicinity 
</p>
<p>of g = g Lunar. 
</p>
<p>Furthermore, in order to facilitate the convenient 
</p>
<p>donning of space suits, future explorers of the Moon 
</p>
<p>and Mars are likely to live in atmospheres at sub-
</p>
<p>atmospheric pressure but higher oxygen concentration 
</p>
<p>compared to standard air Consequently, partial-g fire 
</p>
<p>in an extraterrestrial habitat is a real hazard that is 
</p>
<p>expected to be substantially worse than in 0-g and 
</p>
<p>potentially worse than even 1-g. 
</p>
<p>Discussion: This work contributes directly to 
</p>
<p>filling knowledge and capability gaps in spacecraft fire 
</p>
<p>safety that have been the focus of many projects at the 
</p>
<p>NASA John H. Glenn Research Center. NASA has 
</p>
<p>undertaken extensive tests examining solid fuel flame 
</p>
<p>spread with a particular application to material 
</p>
<p>flammability in microgravity culminating in a number 
</p>
<p>of flight experiments (e.g., [3-7]). Partial gravity tests 
</p>
<p>can be performed in drop towers [8] and aboard 
</p>
<p>aircraft [2], but the available times are too short to 
</p>
<p>provide significant flame development (5 s for drop 
</p>
<p>tower and ~25 s for aircraft). For an orbital platform 
</p>
<p>such as the International Space Station (ISS), partial 
</p>
<p>gravity tests can be performed using centrifuges, but 
</p>
<p>those currently available have small radii of rotation 
</p>
<p>producing appreciable Coriolis effects for the length 
</p>
<p>scales necessary, adding complexity to the 
</p>
<p>interpretation of results. A sounding rocket utilizing 
</p>
<p>vehicle rotation to simulate Lunar gravity provided test 
</p>
<p>times on the order of 150 s, burning two different 
</p>
<p>materials, but again the Coriolis force introduces 
</p>
<p>uncertainty. 
</p>
<p>Combustion on the Moon: The first-ever 
</p>
<p>combustion experiments on another world will be 
</p>
<p>performed, with a planned launch date of late 2026. 
</p>
<p>Flammability of Materials on the Moon (FM2) will 
</p>
<p>burn a total of four solid fuel samples in quiescent 
</p>
<p>habitable atmospheres in actual long duration Lunar 
</p>
<p>gravity (Fig. 1). Cameras will be used to record flame 
</p>
<p>characteristics, including spread rate. Radiometers and 
</p>
<p>an oxygen sensor will be included. The tests will 
</p>
<p>provide benchmark data and are part of the larger </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>effort to understand how Lunar gravity will affect 
</p>
<p>material flammability. 
</p>
<p>Direct Lunar access offers the best way to assess 
</p>
<p>flammability of materials on the Moon. An extensive 
</p>
<p>materials qualification test series there would be ideal, 
</p>
<p>but such tests will need to be put off until an extended 
</p>
<p>human presence on the Moon is established. Prior to 
</p>
<p>this, a limited series of tests would enable a 
</p>
<p>comparison between fires on the Moon and Earth and 
</p>
<p>would validate models and theories developed using 0-
</p>
<p>g and 1-g testing. 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 1. Top: FM2 experiment design showing solid 
</p>
<p>model and cross section of the combustion chamber. 
</p>
<p>Four flat samples are burned in separate tests. Small, 
</p>
<p>pressurized gas bottles provide air for each test. 
</p>
<p>Cameras record flame images while sensors include 
</p>
<p>thermocouples, radiometers, and O2 concentration. 
</p>
<p>Bottom: FM2 Hardware. Left: Chamber exterior; 
</p>
<p>Right: Internal components. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The outcome of this work would include the first-
</p>
<p>ever, long-duration observation and measurement of 
</p>
<p>flame spread in a partial-g environment. It is expected 
</p>
<p>that the fuels tested will provide verification of the 
</p>
<p>hypothesis that some materials burning in partial-g 
</p>
<p>environments may be more flammable compared to 1-
</p>
<p>g. The results will provide a bridge between the 
</p>
<p>current 1-g material screening method and actual 
</p>
<p>behavior in reduced gravity. This will refine 
</p>
<p>understanding of the conditions when the test may not 
</p>
<p>be conservative enough, and likely will lead to an 
</p>
<p>amended test procedure which is safer and more 
</p>
<p>accurate. 
</p>
