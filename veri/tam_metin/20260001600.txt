<p>Spacecraft Fire Safety Predictions using Validated Saffire 
</p>
<p>Model 
Justin Niehaus1, David Urban2, Gary Ruff3, Gordon Berger4 and John Easton5 
</p>
<p>NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>A model developed using Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS) that aimed to determine the effect of a fire in a 
</p>
<p>spacecraft was validated by data collected during the Saffire campaign. The model used inlet and outlet 
</p>
<p>temperatures and CO2 concentrations of the Saffire payload where fire spread was taking place to determine 
</p>
<p>the amount of heat and combustion products that made it into Northrop Grumman’s Cygnus vehicle. The 
</p>
<p>model was then validated using six remote sensors in various places in the vehicle, as well as a far field device 
</p>
<p>(FFD) in the open zenith section that was representative of average vehicle values. The current work focuses 
</p>
<p>on using the model to predict fire safety scenarios. One simulation aimed to determine the fate of HCl, which 
</p>
<p>sticks to surfaces. The model prediction showed that the HCl was removed from the atmosphere rapidly. This 
</p>
<p>compared well against the FFD data in the Saffire VI campaign event where a bottle of 5% HCl was released 
</p>
<p>into the vehicle. An additional simulation where the Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) 
</p>
<p>was shut off once the FFD reached 5 ppm of HCl showed that HCl stayed in the atmosphere considerably 
</p>
<p>longer. Continuing the simulation with the ECLSS activated and after temperatures returned to their initial 
</p>
<p>conditions, resulted in a rapid removal of HCL similar to that observed in the original HCl release scenario 
</p>
<p>model. Finally, a simulation that used the heat release rate from a lithium-ion battery test to determine the 
</p>
<p>effect it would have on a spacecraft was performed. This simulation used a heat addition rate that is 
</p>
<p>considerably higher than what was determined from the burning of solid fuels in the Saffire campaign and 
</p>
<p>hence produced a non-trivial temperature increase in more locations within the vehicle. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>lanning effective responses to a fire on a spacecraft is one of the most impactful ways to reduce the risk to a 
</p>
<p>spacecraft’s mission success and crew safety. Oxygen levels for planned missions will be elevated, only adding 
</p>
<p>to this risk [1-3]. Increased oxygen reduces prebreathe time requirements and complexity for EVA operations, as well 
</p>
<p>as decreases mass required for structural containment due to smaller pressure differentials between cabin and the 
</p>
<p>vacuum of space [3]. Full-scale testing at the planned atmosphere, as implemented in previous space programs [4-6], 
</p>
<p>are a way of quantifying the increased risk. This type of fire data was collected during the Saffire project in a 
</p>
<p>microgravity environment [7-9]. Over six sets of orbital experiments, the Saffire payload burned thermally thick 
</p>
<p>polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) and thin (cotton or solid inflammatory boundary at low-speed fabric (SIBAL)) 
</p>
<p>fuels on Northrop Grumman’s (NG) Cygnus vehicle to study flame spread and fire effects on a space craft in a 
</p>
<p>microgravity environment. These were carried out after Cygnus undocked from a resupply mission to the International 
</p>
<p>Space Station (ISS). 
</p>
<p>A computational model of Saffire VI was previously developed and validated against six remote sensors (RS) in 
</p>
<p>Cygnus that measured temperature and CO2 during burn events in the Saffire campaign [10-13]. The model used the 
</p>
<p>CO2 response or the six RS to validate that the trash and cargo placement in the model provided the appropriate flow 
</p>
<p>paths, as well as the free volume matched the experimental value. The temperature response of the RS closest to the 
</p>
<p>Saffire payload was used to calibrate an effective heat transfer coefficient of the cargo, and validate it against the other 
</p>
<p>RS. The work described in this paper uses the validated computational model to predict other spacecraft fire scenarios 
</p>
<p>not produced in the Saffire experiments. The first scenario being set of simulations explored acid gas transport with 
</p>
<p>the objective of determining the effect flow had on the fate of acid gas in a spacecraft. The second set of simulations 
</p>
<p>involved using experimental data from a lithium-ion battery (LIB) fire, an increasingly relevant fire hazard, with the 
</p>
<p>objective of simulating a more energetic fire than what was provided by the Saffire burn event. 
</p>
<p>A fire in a spacecraft can create acid gases such as HCl from the burning of electronics, which stick to surfaces 
</p>
<p>unlike other combustion products. In addition to the burn events in the Saffire VI experiments, a bottle of HCl was 
</p>
<p>released to study the fate of acid gas in a spacecraft. While not measured at the RS locations, it was measured at the 
</p>
<p>Far Field Device (FFD) in the zenith section of the cabin, which was representative of average cabin values. To 
</p>
<p>complement the microgravity tests, small-scale ground testing was performed to determine the uptake of acid gas by 
</p>
<p>typical spacecraft materials, predominantly treated aluminum [14,15]. This uptake rate was applied to the surfaces of 
</p>
<p>the Saffire model and the transport to the FFD in the model was compared to the experiment. Once a reasonable 
</p>
<p>comparison was observed, additional simulations were performed with the objective to determine the effect on cabin 
</p>
<p>flow on the fate of acid gas. A typical recommendation during a fire in spacecraft is to turn off the flow to prohibit 
</p>
<p>oxygen transport to the flame to encourage extinction [5, 16]. Simulations were run where once HCl reached a 
</p>
<p>threshold value, the environmental control and life support system (ECLSS) flow, as well as any flow associated with 
</p>
<p>the Saffire Flow Unit (SFU) were shut off to see the effect on the fate of HCl. An additional simulation was run where 
</p>
<p>the flow was turned back on once the temperature returned to the initial value. 
</p>
<p>The last set simulation used the heat addition rate (HAR) produced from lithium-ion battery (LIB) tests with the 
</p>
<p>objective of predicting a relevant fire hazard and a more energetic fire than that produced by the burning of SIBAL 
</p>
<p>or PMMA would have on a spacecraft. The heat released into Cygnus from the burn events of Saffire proved to not 
</p>
<p>increase the temperature in the vehicle significantly. The ground tests involving LIB fires provided a larger max HAR 
</p>
<p>and more overall heat than the Saffire burn events, and hence a hotter cabin. 
</p>
<p>II. Experiments 
</p>
<p>A. Saffire Microgravity Experiments 
</p>
<p>The Saffire campaign of experiments performed large-scale burn events to study the smoke plume and combustion 
</p>
<p>product transport within an orbiting vehicle [7-9]. All six of these set of experiments took place in between separate 
</p>
<p>resupply missions to the ISS and controlled burn up in the atmosphere, giving approximately three days of 
</p>
<p>microgravity combustion experimentation and data downlink. Saffire VI, which is the focus of this paper, took place 
</p>

<p>in December of 2023. The pressure vessel, which is approximately 3 meters in diameter by 4 meters in length, also 
</p>
<p>contained the environmental control and life support system (ECLSS) for maintaining flow conditions, and various 
</p>
<p>instrumentation for measuring temperature and combustion product concentrations throughout the vehicle. Figure 1 
</p>
<p>gives a schematic of the Cygnus vehicle and Saffire experiments. 
</p>
<p>Cargo and trash were placed in Cygnus to create channels to transport heat and combustion products from the 
</p>
<p>outlet of Saffire in the nadir section to the zenith section of the spacecraft. This created four different channels, similar 
</p>
<p>to the forward port standoff illustrated in Figure 1. Each channel had an RS that measured temperature and combustion 
</p>
<p>products, such as CO2. In order to apply heat and combustion products into the domain, or the gas volume of the 
</p>
<p>model, a HAR and a mass generation rate (MGR) are applied to the 50 cm2 SFU outlet that flows hot air into the 
</p>
<p>Cygnus vehicle.

</p>

</p>
<p>and help determine the fate of acid gases after a spacecraft fire. Specifically, HCl uptake onto treated aluminum 
</p>
<p>surfaces has been measured in past studies [14, 15]. Figure 2 gives a schematic of the experimental setup. A bottle of 
</p>
<p>dry air and a bottle of approximately 50 parts per million (ppm) of HCl balanced with nitrogen were used to flow a </p>

<p>C. Lithium-Ion Battery Ground Testing 
</p>
<p>An experiment was set up to determine the heat and product release of a LIB cell pack during thermal runaway. The 
</p>
<p>apparatus included an outer enclosure that was connected to room ventilation air flow 2.29 m tall by 2.44 m in width 
</p>
<p>and depth. Inside the outer enclosure was an inner enclosure where the battery cell pack was ignited with two hot 
</p>
<p>plates. The inner enclosure connected to a measurement duct that was 76 mm in diameter and probed the air stream 
</p>
<p>for O2, CO2, CO concentration and temperature. Figure 3 shows a schematic of the experimental apparatus. 
</p>
<p>The data used to provide the HAR for the Saffire model was collected from the burning of a 97 Watt-hour, 6 LIB 
</p>
<p>cell pack. The batteries were at their fully charged state. The cells were ignited with two 500 W strip heaters, each 
</p>
<p>igniting three of the cells. The measurement duct was designed and built to allow for real-time calculation of mass 
</p>
<p>flow rate, temperature, static pressure, and combustion products using ISO standard 5167. Two measurement devices, 
</p>
<p>each with their own pump to draw in a sample of gas through the air stream, determined oxygen, CO2 and CO 
</p>
<p>concentrations. A HEPA filter and a cold trap filtered out particulates before concentration measurements. The flow 
</p>
<p>through the measurement duct for the test used in the model was 1.99 m3/min. The HAR that was used in the model 
</p>
<p>was determined with oxygen consumption calorimetry, which used an energy release of 13.1 MJ/kg of O2 consumed. 
</p>
<p>More information on the development of the test cell, and oxygen consumption calorimetry measurements can be 
</p>
<p>found in work performed by Easton et al. [18].

<p>III. Modeling 
</p>
<p> A previously developed model of the Cygnus vehicle during the Saffire burn events was used to predict the effect 
</p>
<p>of a fire inside a spacecraft. This model uses Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS), which is a large-eddy turbulent code 
</p>
<p>developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [19]. Placement of cargo were discussed in 
</p>
<p>previous work [13]. Table 1 gives the initial atmospheric conditions for all models, as well as the flow boundary 
</p>
<p>conditions. 
</p>
<p> For the validated Saffire model, the HAR and MGR were calculated from the SFU inlet and outlet temperature 
</p>
<p>and CO2 concentration experimental data, respectively [13]. The HCl MGR was also determined from experimental 
</p>
<p>data. The LIB HAR was determined from oxygen consumption calorimetry [18]. The CO2 MGR for LIB tests was 
</p>
<p>calculated from CO2 measurements in the measurement duct, but is not the focus of this paper. These values in the 
</p>
<p>various simulations are applied to the outlet of the SFU, which is a thermally inert object that intakes cool cabin air 
</p>
<p>and outputs combustion products and hot air. Figure 4 gives a schematic of how the HAR and MGR are applied to 
</p>
<p>the SFU and input heat and combustion products into Cygnus. The graphs in Figure 4 represent the HAR and MGR 
</p>
<p>that are used in the LIB simulations. No combustion or flame properties were used in the model. The same convective 
</p>
<p>heat transfer coefficient of 150 W/m2-K that was calibrated to experimental data in previous work was applied to the 
</p>
<p>surfaces of the cargo in these simulations [15]. 
</p>

<p> A strategy for extinguishing a fire in an enclosed pressure vessel in a microgravity environment is to turn off the 
</p>
<p>flow in a vehicle in order to starve the fire of oxygen. While this can be effective, stagnation of flow can lead to 
</p>
<p>pockets of high concentration of hazardous combustion products. A simulation was run in which the ECLSS, SFU, 
</p>
<p>and avionics flow were turned off once the FFD reached the 1-hour Spacecraft Maximum Allowable Concentration 
</p>
<p>(SMAC) [20] of HCl, which was approximately 90 s for this simulation. A subsequent simulation turned the flow 
</p>
<p>back on once the temperature returned to initial values. 
</p>
<p> The last set of simulations focused on the effect that the increased HAR provided by the LIB had on the vehicle. 
</p>
<p>The temperature at the RS locations for the LIB simulation are compared to the temperatures of the Saffire VI SIBAL 
</p>
<p>burn event. Additional simulations were completed that assigned material thermal properties to the cargo to see if the 
</p>
<p>surfaces became hot enough to become hazardous. 
</p>
<p>IV. Results and Discussion 
</p>
<p>The concentration of HCl from the bottle release in Saffire VI SIBAL at the FFD is shown in Figure 6 against the 
</p>
<p>model prediction. The peak concentration of 32 ppm and time to peak of 100 s match well with experimental results. 
</p>
<p>This indicates that the bulk cabin flow and the release of the HCl bottle from the SFU in the simulation match the 
</p>
<p>experiment. The drop in concentration happened significantly faster in the experiment than in the model. There are a 
</p>
<p>couple reasons this discrepancy might exist. First, there was a lot of surface area in the FFD, as well as losses from 
</p>
<p>the combustion product monitor that are not accounted for in the model which could add further decay. This is similar 
</p>
<p>to the explanation of lower temperature in the FFD experiments compared to simulations during previous model 
</p>
<p>development [10]. Second, the heat transfer coefficient that was calibrated during previous model development [11] 
</p>
<p>is representative of an effective heat transfer coefficient to account for surfaces that are not perfectly rectangular or 
</p>
<p>smooth, but amorphous, rough, and porous, hence underestimating the real surface area participating in heat transfer, 
</p>
<p>and likely mass transfer. This increased surface area, as well as mixed materials of the surface would lend itself to the 
</p>
<p>same type of calibration of an effective uptake rate as the effective heat transfer coefficient if a more accurate 
</p>
<p>prediction is needed. For this work, the simulation compares well enough with experimental data that it can be used 
</p>
<p>to explore the effect of flow on the fate of acid gas. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>
<p>standoff where the volume to surface area was much higher, allowing for more uptake and hence more removal of 
</p>
<p>HCl from the atmosphere. RS6 was located in a more open area, away from surfaces that uptake HCl. In that case, the 
</p>
<p>HCl stayed in the atmosphere surrounding RS6 until the flow is turned back on.

<p>To explore additional hazards in regard to increased surface temperature, additional simulations were run where 
</p>
<p>surface thermal properties were also assigned to cargo in the channels. Steel and aluminum were first chosen as 
</p>
<p>realistic metals, despite the bags of trash and cargo likely having multiple materials. Table 2 shows the material 
</p>
<p>properties used. Figure 14 shows the surface temperature at the surface directly below RS3. In comparing the two 
</p>
<p>metals, the increased thermal diffusivity of the aluminum transported heat away from the surface and into the cool 
</p>
<p>center, while heat stayed longer at the surface of the steel. The increased thermal mass of the steel corresponds to a 
</p>
<p>lower steady state temperature. In any case, the temperature did not reach above 0.5 °C of the initial temperature, 
</p>
<p>showing that surface temperature was not likely to be a critical hazard to the crew beyond surfaces directly in contact 
</p>
<p>with a flame. This is also an indication that using an isothermal boundary condition at surfaces will in most cases 
</p>
<p>provide a good prediction of the conditions of the atmosphere inside the vehicle. 
</p>
<p>The material was then changed to foam to demonstrate how hot a material with a much lower thermal mass could 
</p>
<p>become. Foam is present in Cygnus, as well as being an available material in the Pyrosim library. The surface reached 
</p>
<p>45 °C but returned to its initial temperature by 200 s. Surface temperatures reaching 60 °C, as cited by ASTM standard 
</p>
<p>C1055-03 [22], are often deemed hazardous. While the amount of heat added to the vehicle in these simulations did 
</p>
<p>not produce surfaces hot enough to be hazardous, tests are planned to burn entire laptops and multiple battery packs. 
</p>
<p>These tests could produce a larger and more sustained burn event, in which case the surface temperature could become 
</p>
<p>a risk factor.

<p>V. Conclusions 
</p>
<p> A previously validated model of the Cygnus vehicle during the burn events of the Saffire experimental campaign 
</p>
<p>was used to simulate other fire scenarios of interest. First, an HCl MGR was added to the Saffire VI SIBAL model 
</p>
<p>that was provided experimentally and an HCl uptake rate that was experimentally measured was implemented at the 
</p>
<p>cargo of the vehicle. The simulated prediction of the HCl at the FFD matched the peak of the experimental data, with 
</p>
<p>some deviations at the tail end of the response. Next, the flow in the vehicle was turned off once HCl reached 5 ppm 
</p>
<p>at the FFD, which greatly reduced the rate of uptake and left high concentrations of HCl in the vehicle for extended 
</p>
<p>periods of time. An additional simulation where the flow was turned back on after the temperature returned to baseline 
</p>
<p>showed that the rate of uptake of HCl returned to the previous rate. The next set of simulations focused on the heat 
</p>
<p>transfer from a LIB fire, and the results were compared to the previous SIBAL simulation. As expected, the increased 
</p>
<p>HAR provided by the LIB produced higher temperatures for the remote sensors that were in the flow path of Saffire. 
</p>
<p>Simulations where the material were changed from isothermal to either steel or aluminum and measured the surface 
</p>
<p>temperature of the cargo provided an insignificant difference. Future experiments could provide heat and product 
</p>
<p>release of multicell LIBs with entire laptops and battery fire bags burned that would provide even larger heat release 
</p>
<p>for a sustained time. The burning of these LIBs, as well as other electronics, will create other acid gases that will need 
</p>
<p>to be further investigated to determine their fate in a spacecraft. 
</p>
