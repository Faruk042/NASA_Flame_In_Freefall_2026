<p>Abstract
</p>
<p>A simplified chemical-kinetic cool-flame mechanism for n-alkanes has recently been updated and applied to the
description of the structure of a freely propagating cool premixed flame controlled by this chemistry, resulting in
formulas for calculating the corresponding laminar burning velocity. Although comparisons with experiments were
made in that investigation, reasonably accurate data were lacking to test the predicted burning velocities carefully.
More accurate data are, however, available on burning rates of such cool flames with ozone addition employed
to enhance stability. In the present work, two additional steps associated with ozone are appended to the latest
simplified chemistry, and predicted flame temperatures and laminar burning velocities are calculated and are found
to agree with the measured flame temperatures within about 50 K or better and (with a few exceptions) to agree
with the measured burning velocities within a factor of 2.
</p>
<p>Keywords: Premixed cool flames, Laminar burning velocity, N-alkanes, Simplified chemical kinetics
</p>
<p>∗Corresponding author: Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, Case Western Reserve University,
Cleveland, Ohio 44106, USA
</p>
<p>Novelty and significance statement
</p>
<p>A compact nine-step chemical kinetic model for
n-alkane cool flames, with rate parameters ob-
tained from a detailed mechanism, to predict ozone-
enhanced laminar burning velocities is presented.
The theoretical model that employs activation energy
asymptotics is first validated against numerical sim-
ulations with semi-detailed chemical kinetics. Theo-
retical calculations of ozone-assisted premixed cool-
flame propagation speeds for propane, n-heptane,
and n-decane are shown to compare reasonably well
with earlier experimental measurements, demonstrat-
ing the effectiveness of the simplified theory.
</p>
<p>1. Introduction
</p>
<p>Cool-flame chemistry occurs at temperatures
within and below the temperature region (entirely be-
low 1000 K) over which a negative temperature coef-

<p>ficient (NTC, induction time increasing with increas-
ing temperature) is observed during autoignition of
normal alkanes [1]. Measurements of the combus-
tion and extinction of normal-alkane droplets sup-
ported by cool-flame chemistry [2], performed in the
International Space Station (ISS), revealed a stage of
quasi-steady burning supported by cool-flame chem-
istry, and, in order to describe this stage as simply
as possible, a maximally simplified chemical-kinetic
description (developed beginning with the San Diego
mechanism [3]) was employed to represent the cool
diffusion-flame structure and to calculate its extinc-
tion conditions [4, 5, 6]. The reader is referred to the
previous publication [2] for further background infor-
mation on cool flames.
</p>
<p>That simplified chemistry was later applied to pre-
dict structures and propagation velocities of premixed
cool flames [7], which were observed in recently pub-
lished ground-based normal gravity [8], and micro-
gravity [9] droplet autoignition experiments. Exper-
imentally, these cool premixed flames initially begin
to propagate at a constant velocity, in approximate
agreement with the predicted velocity, but they began</p>

</p>
<p>to accelerate towards hot-flame velocities as they pro-
gressed. Foster and Pearlman [10] have reported ob-
servations of unsteady, premixed cool flame propaga-
tion in equimolar propane/oxygen mixtures in a spher-
ical static reactor under microgravity at initial temper-
atures around 600 K. This unsteady behavior makes it
difficult to obtain accurate comparisons between pre-
dictions and measurements. There are, however, ex-
periments involving ozone addition to the mixture,
in which premixed cool flames are stabilized on a
flat-flame burner [11], [12], [13], [14]. The aim of
the present work is to determine whether the simpli-
fied kinetic mechanism can be augmented by addition
of suitable ozone-related steps to describe the flames
with ozone addition and, if so, to test whether that
augmentation can improve tendencies towards static
stability. To facilitate that augmentation, ozone and
its necessary steps have recently been added to the San
Diego mechanism [3].
</p>
<p>The remainder of the paper is organized as fol-
lows. First a nine-step chemical kinetic mechanism
is presented, followed by the formulation of the pre-
mixed cool-flame propagation problem, and the ar-
rangement of asymptotic reaction zones. An ana-
lytical solution for the ozone concentration profile
is presented next. Since the formulation and the
solution techniques closely follow the recently de-
veloped seven-step premixed cool-flame propagation
model [7], only the modifications introduced by the
addition of ozone in the fresh mixture are presented.
Section 6 describes changes resulting from the ozone
addition. Before comparing the predictions of the
asymptotic model with experimental data, the model
is tested against numerical computations with “de-
tailed" chemistry for normal propane. Comprehen-
sive comparisons with experimental data for propane,
n-heptane and n-decane, are then presented and dis-
cussed, followed by concluding remarks summarizing
the key findings.
</p>
<p>2. The Nine-Step Reduced Chemistry
</p>
<p>The chemical reactions in the simplified descrip-
tion addressed here are shown in Table 1, where F de-
notes the normal alkane, R is its alkyl radical, I repre-
sents the most relevant species resulting from the first
oxygen addition to R (generally denoted as QOOH),
and K stands for the associated alkylketohydroperox-
ide. In the table, the P’s stand for collections of prod-
uct species that do not have to be considered, and the
last two steps have been added to the previous seven
[7] to account for the influence of ozone. The San
Diego mechanism [3] is selected as the detailed mech-
anism from which the reduced mechanism is derived.
According to this mechanism, the rate parameters for
step 8 have been taken from previous work [15], while
the value for step 9 is an estimate based on recent lit-
erature. For n-heptane, the values used are the same
as in a previous publication [6] which again is based
on the San Diego mechanism. Because the San Diego
</p>
<p>mechanism does not include detailed chemistry for n-
decane, the rate parameters are estimated following
the approach in [5], scaling the values based on the
number of relevant isomers (5 decane isomers versus
4 heptane isomers).
</p>
<p>In this description, following hydrogen abstraction
from the original fuel by hydroxyl or the oxygen atom,
the second step presumes rapid isomerization follow-
ing the first oxygen-molecule addition. The third step
was derived from steady-state approximations for the
species formed by the second oxygen-molecule addi-
tion and for its isomerizations, resulting in maintain-
ing partial equilibrium for its re-dissociation, and the
fourth step completes the low-temperature branching,
having generated two hydroxyl radicals for the one
initially consumed. The next two steps are needed
to slow down this process by removing intermediates,
thereby providing improved accuracy, and the seventh
step affects the flame structure by confining the chem-
istry to a narrow region around the flame. The im-
portance of the seventh step in the context of the San
Diego mechanism is further explained in [7]. The ef-
fect of ozone is to elevate the importance of O in H-
abstraction from F through the last step, thereby aug-
menting the rate of production of hydroxyl, which in-
creases the low-temperature branching, speeding up
that rate.
</p>
<p>As in the previous descriptions, both R and OH
maintain accurate chemical-kinetic steady-state ap-
proximations. In the most recent work [7], this led
to an effectively five-step formulation, but with ozone
addition this increases to six steps, the accuracy of
an O-atom steady state being even better than that for
OH; it is the elimination of O from the last two steps
that results in the addition of only one more step in
the formulation. After the rates of the first and last
steps are eliminated by introduction of the steady-
state approximations for OH and O, the steady-state
expression for R indicates that the net rate of the I-
production becomes (ω4 −ω5 −ω6 −ω7 +2ω8), where
ω j is the rate step j in Table 1. The ozone addition
thus promotes I production through the addition of
step 8. As before [7], the amount of heat release is
considered to be an empirically adjustable parame-
ter, the rate of heat release being proportional to the
rate of step 4 (chain branching step). Throughout the
present paper, that heat release is characterized by the
constant value of 2000 K for the temperature TQ that
measures the heat release, selected for all cases con-
sidered, rather than making finer adjustments individ-
ually. Decreasing this value decreases the predicted
flame temperature somewhat and the predicted lam-
inar burning velocity appreciably, leading to overall
poorer agreements.
</p>

<p>Boundary conditions are applied upstream (sub-
script u) at ξ = −∞ and downstream (subscript f )
at ξ = ∞. The values Tu, YFu, YO2u, and YO3u spec-
ify the mixture into which the flame is propagating,
which may be lean, rich, or stoichiometric, with nor-
mal, diluted, or enriched air, while the intermediate
mass fractions YI and YK must vanish at upstream
infinity. The final temperature T f is the flame tem-
perature, which is approached at downstream infinity,
but its value is determined by the flame structure and
is not known in advance. Similarly, since both fuel
and oxygen leak through the flame, the downstream
values YF f and YO2 f depend on the flame structure.
The downstream boundary conditions for T and these
leaking chemical species therefore are simply zero-
gradient conditions, while the intermediate mass frac-
tions YI and YK must vanish at downstream infinity,
just as they do upstream.
</p>
<p>4. Reaction Zones
</p>
<p>The arrangement of the reaction zones is the same
as was described previously [7]. The large value of Ta
for step 4 led to that being the thinnest zone, and, in
the approximations that were introduced, that was the
only zone in which heat was released, fuel and oxygen
were consumed, and the intermediate I was produced,
while K was consumed completely there. For fuel and
oxygen, in particular, as well as for K, this involved
assuming that ω3, and ω6 were small compared with
ω4 there, and, with ozone addition, this also requires
ω8 to be negligible compared with ω4 there, a con-
dition that is maintained at the relatively small ozone
concentrations of interest. Activation-energy asymp-
totics was applied to this heat-release zone.
</p>
<p>Downstream from this thin heat-release zone, to
leading order YK is zero, and T , YF , and YO2 are con-
stant, resulting in YI decaying unimolecularly, from
its value, YI f , in the thin reaction zone where it is pro-
duced, to zero at downstream infinity, at the constant
reaction rates of step 5 and step 6, as in [7]. Note that
in step 6 and elsewhere, we have expressed the con-
centration of R in terms of I as [R] = [I](A3/A2)e−T3/T ,
using the steady-sate approximations for R and OH.
</p>
<p>Upstream of the thin heat-release zone, in the lead-
ing order, the I mass fraction remains constant at the
value of YI f , while the derivative dYI/dξ changes as
in [7]. The I consumption, however, occurs at a rate
(ω5 + ω6 − 2ω8) due to the presence of ozone which
produces R through step 9, which equal to step 8 un-
der O steady state. This term is treated as a con-
sumption term for sufficiently small values of ozone
concentrations in the fresh mixture. It may be seen
from the values of Ta listed in the table that the activa-
tion energy for the ozone-consumption step 8 is very
close to that of step 5, so that it becomes reasonable
to assume that ozone decomposition also occurs in
what originally was this thin upstream I-consumption
zone. The presence of ω8 in the chemical source term
for I then implies that this I-consumption zone may
become an I-production zone, instead, if a sufficient</p>

<p>7. Comparison of Reduced and Detailed Chem-
istry
</p>
<p>Propane is selected as the fuel for which the de-
tailed and reduced mechanisms are compared because
it is the normal alkane for which the San Diego mech-
anism is most complete. For that fuel, the full chem-
</p>
<p>istry in the mechanism involves 271 steps among 59
species, so the 9 steps among the 13 species in the re-
duced mechanism represents a very substantial reduc-
tion. The computations for freely-propagating flames
with the detailed chemistry were performed using
Cantera [16]. When the full mechanism is employed,
the solutions always converge to the hot-flame struc-
ture, preventing the cool flame from being captured.
Deletions in the mechanism therefore have to be made
to recover the cool flame. Earlier computational work
has demonstrated that cool flames produce only small
amounts of CO and trace amounts of CO2 [17]. Those
two species therefore can be deleted from the chem-
istry while still obtaining sufficiently accurate descrip-
tions of cool-flame structures. The result is a mecha-
nism of 226 reactions among 57 species. For fuel-
lean flames, the resulting carbon-containing products
are then only aldehydes, consistent with the dominant
products found previously. Figure 1, pertaining to one
of the experimental conditions [11], shows a repre-
sentative computed flame structure. It is seen in this
figure that, while the ozone is completely used up and
the fuel is substantially depleted in the millimeter-size
flame, there is very little depletion of oxygen, and the
flame temperature remains well below 1000 K, as is
needed for the cool flame.
</p>

<p>8. Comparisons of Predictions with Experiment
</p>
<p>The experiments with ozone addition employed a
Hencken flat-flame burner that stabilizes a flat flame
at its outlet [11]. Since the pressure, and the flow
rate are controlled experimentally, they can be kept
low enough to avoid the occurrence of hot flames,
</p>
<p>and the cool-flame burning velocity can then be deter-
mined by gradually increasing the flow rate until the
burner-attached cool flame lifts off. Experimental re-
sults were reported for normal propane [11], heptane
[12, 14], and decane [13].
</p>
<p>Figure 3 compares the predictions of the nine-step
chemistry with the experiment for flame temperatures
and burning velocities as functions of pressure for the
reported propane measurements [11]. In the experi-
mental results reported, the equivalence ratio was de-
creased to 0.17 at the lowest pressures, as indicated
in the figure. The agreements are seen to be reason-
able except at the lowest pressures, corresponding to
the smallest equivalence ratios, where the predicted
burning velocities lie well below the measured val-
ues. Under those conditions the reduced chemistry
likely is very inaccurate because the associated pre-
dictions also lie below those of the detailed chemistry,
although that, too, (not shown) predicts burning ve-
locities well below the measurements. It is curious
that the measured flame speeds decrease as the mea-
sured flame temperatures increase, which is counter-
intuitive and does not appear to be predicted by any
existing mechanism.
</p>
<p>At the lowest pressures, the ozone mole fraction
is nearly twice that of the fuel, so the system is ap-
proaching one of an ozone decomposition flame. One
would think that any ozone contribution would not in-
crease the burning velocity without also increasing the
flame temperature, contrary to what is observed in the
experiments at different pressures, but the influence
of the ozone chemistry is unclear. As has often been
remarked recently, the chemistry of ozone decomposi-
tion flames suffers from a number of uncertainties and
would benefit from additional study, not having been
addressed in detail recently; for example, a possible
autocatalytic influence of O atoms on ozone decom-
position may be responsible for the high burning ve-
locities seen in the experiment at low pressures. The
reduced chemistry is designed to describe influences
of ozone on normal-alkane cool flames and would not
correctly predict structures of ozone decomposition
flames, which would require additional steps to be in-
cluded in the mechanism, some of which may not be
known, so the large discrepancy may be understand-
able as a consequence of an improper description of
the ozone effects under those conditions. The large
discrepancies at the low-pressure, low-equivalence-
ratio conditions thus may be attributed to deficiencies
in the description of the ozone effects rather than lim-
itations of the experimental measurements.
</p>

<p>9. Conclusions
</p>
<p>It may be concluded from these studies that, be-
sides providing a simplified understanding of cool-
flame structures, the greatly reduced chemistry de-
veloped here will produce agreement with measured
flame temperatures and burning velocities for pre-
mixed cool flames that is close enough for many pur-
poses. The key intermediate, alkylketohydroperoxide,
carries the cool-flame structure, forming the reaction
products, aldehydes and water, that raise the temper-
ature of the mixture to values above 500 K but below
1000 K. Cool premixed flames are unique in that their
essential chemistry is entirely different from that of
other premixed flames, thereby raising interest in their
further study for future applications, including possi-
ble production of novel chemicals and use when there
are requirements for low-level heating rates.
</p>
