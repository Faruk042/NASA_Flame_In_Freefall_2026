<p>Vehicle Modeling During the Burn Events in the Saffire VI 
</p>
<p>Experiments 
Justin Niehaus1, John Brooker2, David Urban3, Gary Ruff4 
</p>
<p>NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>In order to design a fire-safe environment inside a spacecraft, knowledge of the transport of heat and 
</p>
<p>combustion products in a reduced gravity environment is needed. After a resupply mission to the ISS, the 
</p>
<p>Saffire project provided large-scale fire data in a microgravity environment inside of Northrup Grumman’s 
</p>
<p>Cygnus vehicle. Solid materials were burned, including a thin cotton-based sample and thick thermoplastic 
</p>
<p>samples, to study a large-scale fire’s effect on a spacecraft. Sensors were placed in Cygnus that measured 
</p>
<p>temperature and species concentration at four different standoffs, as well as nadir and zenith locations. 
</p>
<p>Pyrosim was used to develop a model to predict the three burn events from Saffire VI using inlet and outlet 
</p>
<p>data from the Saffire payload. The convective heat transfer coefficient between the cargo and the cabin air 
</p>
<p>that was previously calibrated in the Saffire V model was used to predict the temperature response in the 
</p>
<p>Saffire VI burn events. Good predictions of the CO2 and temperature responses for the remote sensors 
</p>
<p>throughout Cygnus were realized. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>ire events present some of the most catastrophic risks to a spacecraft. Previous programs such as Apollo and 
</p>
<p>Skylab have undergone full-scale fire testing to help quantify this risk1-3. It is also standard practice in ground 
</p>
<p>systems and submarines4,5. As planned oxygen levels in spacecraft begin to rise, it is important to have full-scale fire 
</p>
<p>tests to help determine the spread of heat and combustion products. The Saffire project has provided such data in a 
</p>
<p>microgravity environment. The sixth campaign, which took place on Northrop Grumman’s (NG) Cygnus vehicle 
</p>
<p>after undocking from a resupply mission to the ISS, burned a cotton-based fabric, two Polymethyl (Methacrylate) 
</p>
<p>(PMMA) samples and attempted to burn a Nomex fabric. The data collected from these tests, such as flame 
</p>
<p>temperature and spread velocity will be useful to combustion scientists and fire-safety engineers in designing a safer 
</p>
<p>spacecraft. 
</p>
<p>The first three Saffire campaigns focused primarily on the large-scale flame spread inside the Saffire payload6-9, 
</p>
<p>while the next three Saffire campaigns expanded the experiments to investigate the effect of a fire on the spacecraft. 
</p>
<p>Sensors were placed throughout the vehicle to measured temperature and species concentration, such as CO2. These 
</p>
<p>far-field gas and particle sensors will provide data that will increase understanding of smoke plume and combustion 
</p>
<p>product transport within an orbiting vehicle10-13. In addition to the burn events, the test campaign includes flow 
</p>
<p>visualization events, CO2 scrubs, and gas releases. Six remote sensors (RS) were utilized in Saffire IV-VI to 
</p>
<p>measure temperature and product concentration at various locations in the cabin. A far-field device (FFD) was also 
</p>
<p>placed in the zenith section, representing the cabin’s average temperature and species concentrations. All Saffire IV 
</p>
<p>through VI campaigns used a cotton-based fuel and PMMA for the burn events. 
This paper presents the development of a model of the Cygnus vehicle during the Saffire VI experimental 
</p>
<p>campaign and the simulation of the three burn events. The vehicle’s geometry and the trash and cargo placement 
</p>
<p>after the resupply mission were determined from a map provided by NG, as well as photographs taken by the ISS 
</p>
<p>crew. Inlet and outlet sensor data from the Saffire payload was used to calculate the heat input and species 
</p>
<p>generation rate from Saffire to the Cygnus vehicle. The effective convective heat transfer between the cargo and the 
</p>
<p>cabin air that was calibrated in the work describing model development of Saffire V is also used in this work14. The 
</p>
<p>primary verification mechanism of the model was temperature and CO2 data provided at the RS locations. 
</p>
<p>Developing verified models using full-scale fire tests is an excellent way to gain knowledge of the unknown and 
</p>
<p>move towards a safer environment in space. 
</p>
<p>II. Saffire Experiment 
</p>

</p>
<p>ISS. Figure 1 shows a schematic of the Saffire experiment within the Cygnus vehicle without the trash and cargo

<p>that form standoffs for heat and combustion product transfer. Vital instrumentation and the vehicle Environmental 
</p>
<p>Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) are illustrated. The size of the pressure vessel is nearly 3 meters in 
</p>
<p>diameter by 4 meters in length.

<p> Nine events occurred during the Saffire VI experimental campaign, including the three burn events that are 
</p>
<p>simulated in this work. These events are described in Table 1. Similar to previous Saffire campaigns, Saffire VI 
</p>
<p>started with pulling cabin air through the FFD to scrub the vehicle of CO2. A scrub was also performed during event 
</p>
<p>7, between the two PMMA burn events. During event 2, CO2 was released into the vehicle to calculate the free 
</p>
<p>volume in the cabin and aid in analyzing the O2 that would need to be released during the burn events. HCl was also 
</p>
<p>released during event 2 to study acid gas adsorption to surfaces. After all the planned burns were completed, a flow 
</p>
<p>visualization test was performed during 9 to determine any major differences in the Saffire Flow Unit (SFU) from 
</p>
<p>event 3. An attempt to burn Nomex occurred during event 4, but no ignition was successful. Event 5 burned SIBAL 
</p>
<p>fabric at 31.0% O2 and is the first event simulated in this work. This work also simulates the two-sided PMMA burn 
</p>
<p>and one-sided PMMA burn in events 6 and 8, respectively. The initial atmospheric conditions for the experimental 
</p>
<p>campaign at the start of the burn events were 54.1 kPA and 31.0% oxygen, with a temperature of approximately 
</p>
<p>24.3°C. 
</p>
<p>III. Modeling 
</p>
<p>A model was developed to predict the effect of the three burn events of the Cygnus vehicle by utilizing the Fire 
</p>
<p>Dynamics Simulator (FDS), a code developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). This 
</p>
<p>code is optimized for large-eddy turbulent simulations and for low-speed flows usually found in compartment 
</p>
<p>fires17. The graphical interface that was used to develop the vehicle model is Pyrosim TM18. Figure 2 provides a 
</p>
<p>picture of this model, highlighting the important flow boundary conditions and sensors. This geometry was </p>
<p></p>

<p>comprised of a cargo placement map and photographs provided by NG. The mesh size was 0.05 m. A mesh 
</p>
<p>sensitivity analysis was completed to determine the effect of mesh size on accuracy of results. 
</p>
<p>The model was primarily compared to the experiments using the CO2 and temperature readings at the locations 
</p>
<p>of the six remote sensors. All six of these sensors can be seen in Figure 1, while the cargo hides RS2 and RS5 in 
</p>
<p>Figure 2, which has also been described in detail in previous papers about Saffire IV and V14-16. The initial 
</p>
<p>temperature of the cabin used for each simulation was the initial temperature of RS3 since that was closest to the 
</p>
<p>Saffire outlet, which created the greatest response for comparison. Since the initial temperature of all the remote 
</p>
<p>sensors was different in the experiment, the other remote sensor simulation curves were adjusted after the simulation 
</p>
<p>to match the initial temperature of the experimental curves. 
</p>
<p> The flow rates used for the boundary conditions in the simulations are provided in Table 2, which are provided 
</p>
<p>experimentally. The SFU used a flow rate of 0.028 m3/s to give a flow of approximately 20 cm/s over the samples 
</p>
<p>during times of burning but was ramped down at certain times to promote flame extinguishment. The heat from the 
</p>
<p>burn event is captured at the outlet of the Cygnus vehicle by the HAR, which is calculated using the inlet and outlet 
</p>
<p>temperature of the SFU. The HAR is calculated using Eq. 1 and demonstrated in Figure 3 using data from event 5. 
</p>
<p>Since no flame or combustion properties are inputted into the model, the SFU simply acts as an object that intakes 
</p>
<p>cool cabin air and outputs combustion products and hot air. Radiation from the SFU has been assumed to be 
</p>
<p>negligible. The initial atmospheric conditions of the Cygnus vehicle are shown in Table 3. The density and specific 
</p>
<p>heat of the air used to calculate the HAR are taken at the initial temperature since the outlet of the SFU does not 
</p>
<p>exceed 75°C during any burn. Density and specific heat would also be negligibly affected by the increase in main 
</p>
<p>combustion products CO2 and H2O in the atmosphere, which reaches no more than 2% during any burn event.

<p>IV. Results and Discussion 
</p>
<p> The initial experimental RS CO2 values for the SIBAL burn event ranged from 5,078 to 5,704 ppm, giving an 
</p>
<p>average of 5,381 ppm as given by Table 3. This average value was used for the initial cabin CO2 concentration for 
</p>
<p>the event 5 simulation, and then the prediction curves were increased or decreased after the simulation to match the 
</p>
<p>initial experimental values. This method was used consistently for event 6 and 8 as well. Figure 6 shows the 
</p>
<p>experimental CO2 results at the six RS locations against the simulated results. An excellent agreement is realized 
</p>
<p>once the curves reached steady state. This is expected since the CO2 added to the cabin from the MGR (46.5 g) was 
</p>
<p>close to the average CO2 rise predicted from the six remote sensors (46.8 g). However, the rise in the curve is 
</p>
<p>underpredicted by the model at all locations. The largest overshoot of the steady state was RS3 and RS6, which were 
</p>
<p>in the direct path of the Saffire outlet flow. RS2 also had a considerable overshoot in the experimental curve, which 
</p>
<p>was the RS below RS3, indicating that the flow was able to be diverted down into the starboard standoff. It is clear 
</p>
<p>the total amount of CO2 being delivered into the cabin from Saffire is accurate, but how much reaches various parts 
</p>
<p>of the cabin early on during the burn event is inaccurate. If this was a consistent problem among all three burn 
</p>
<p>events, a reasonable conclusion would be that the cargo in the model, especially at the FP standoff needs to be 
</p>
<p>adjusted for more flow to go over RS3. However, as will be discussed later, the CO2 predictions are better during 
</p>
<p>event 6 and 8. </p>

<p>used for this work14,15. Since the remote sensors ranged from 19.9 to 24.3 °C initially, the prediction curves were 
</p>
<p>adjusted so that the initial value matched that of each location to compare the temperature increase. This method was 
</p>
<p>also used for events 6 and 8 as well. The prediction given by the model is in excellent agreement with the 
</p>
<p>experimental model. Besides RS3 and RS6, which are the two in the most direct path of the Saffire outlet flow, there 
</p>
<p>was negligible increase in temperature observed during the burn event compared to the sensitivity of the 
</p>
<p>thermocouples (+/- 0.25 °C). The model predicted this behavior. The total amount of heat input into the Cygnus 
</p>
<p>cabin from Saffire was 124 kJ, which can be calculated by integrating the HAR provided by Eq.1 and Figure 4. This 
</p>
<p>agrees well with the cotton sample in Saffire V that provided 110 kJ of heat into the Cygnus vehicle from the 
</p>
<p>payload. 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>These large-scale experiments and numerical models highlighted the effect a spacecraft fire has on a vehicle, 
</p>
<p>such as heat transfer and product mixing. The results from this work demonstrate the feasibility of predicting a fire’s 
</p>
<p>impact on a spacecraft’s habitability. These verified models from Saffire IV to Saffire VI can be used to predict 
</p>
<p>“worst-case” fire scenarios. For example, heat release rates from experimental calorimetry experiments, such as Li-
</p>
<p>ion batteries, could be provided as input to the model, or the gravity level could be adjusted to study the buoyant 
</p>
<p>effects of heat transfer inside a vehicle under lunar or Martian gravity. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>V. Conclusions 
</p>
<p> The effects of the Saffire burn events on the Cygnus vehicle were revealed using a model developed using FDS. 
</p>
<p>Inlet and outlet sensors to the Saffire payload served as the main inputs into the model, while six remote sensors 
</p>
<p>placed throughout the cabin served as the verification of the model. In previous work, an effective convective heat 
</p>
<p>transfer coefficient of 150 W/m2-K for the cargo in the standoffs that was calibrated at RS3 for the Saffire V cotton 
</p>
<p>burn was used for the three simulations in this work and provided adequate temperature predictions in the cabin. 
</p>
<p>Besides a couple of locations during specific burn events, the model predicted CO2 and temperature throughout the 
</p>
<p>cabin well. These results, and the simulations of the other Saffire campaigns demonstrate the feasibility of predicting 
</p>
<p>a fire’s impact on the habitability of an orbiting spacecraft. With validated models in place, other fire sources, such 
</p>
<p>as Li-ion batteries, can be used to determine the effect of that type of fire in a spacecraft. Similar methods of 
</p>
<p>modeling could also be incorporated with other spacecrafts and gravity levels. </p>
