<p>Computational Fluid Dynamics Analysis of Flame 
Dynamics in Partial Gravity Environments in a 
Rotating Centrifuge 
</p>
<p> 
Ankit Sharmaa,1, Arland Zatania Lojoa,1, Ya-Ting T. Liaoa,*, Paul V. Ferkulb,c, 
Michael C. Johnstonc 
 
aCase Western Reserve University, Cleveland, Ohio 44106, USA 
bUniversities Space Research Association, Brook Park, OH 44142, USA 
</p>
<p>cNASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA 
 
 
______________________________________________________________ 
</p>
<p> 
Abstract 
The safety of spacecraft and crew members is a critical concern for space research and exploration missions. It is 
already known that fire hazards in a microgravity environment present unique challenges, primarily due to the 
absence of buoyancy, which significantly alters fire behavior. The NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to send 
humans to the Moon, introduces a new set of challenges due to the Moon partial gravity compared to the Earth. 
This necessitates a better understanding of fire behavior in partial gravity conditions. However, conducting 
experiments in true partial gravity environments is challenging, and the use of centrifuges to create artificial 
partial gravity introduces complications, including the Coriolis force and limitations in chamber size. 
Consequently, there have been limited studies on flame dynamics in partial gravity. To address these challenges, 
this research employs Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) techniques to investigate flame behavior in a partial 
gravity environment created by a rotating centrifuge. The numerical model is validated against NASA's previous 
experiments and provides information on the effects of the Coriolis force and flow recirculation in the chamber. 
The analysis reveals that the interaction between buoyancy, Coriolis force, and flow recirculation plays a 
significant role in flame behavior. The flame tilt angle observed in both the experiments and the numerical results 
is caused by the combined effects of these forces and their variations along the flame length. In conclusion, this 
research contributes to our understanding of how flames behave in partial gravity environments created by 
rotating centrifuges. It emphasizes the complexity of flame dynamics in such conditions and provides valuable 
insights for future centrifuge experiments, with the goal of improving space exploration safety and understanding 
flame behavior in unique partial gravity environment. 
 
Keywords: Partial gravity; Flames; Coriolis force; Centrifuge.

<p>Novelty and Significance Statement 1 
</p>
<p>Fire behaviours are non-linear to gravity level. 2 
While abundant data exists in normal (Earth) and 3 
microgravity, fire behaviors cannot be interpolated 4 
using ground and microgravity data. Microgravity 5 
centrifuge has emerged as an effective experimental 6 
platform to create partial gravity. This study pioneers 7 
the numerical examination of flame dynamics in 8 
artificial partial gravity created by rotating 9 
centrifuges, revealing how the Coriolis effects and 10 
flow recirculation inside the chamber may complicate 11 
the results. The findings provide insights on how to 12 
interpret existing data and guide the design of future 13 
</p>
<p>centrifuge experiments. Through these, this study 14 
addresses the knowledge gaps in fire dynamics in 15 
partial gravity environment, and contributes to safety 16 
and success of future space exploration. 17 
 18 
1. Introduction 19 
</p>
<p>The safety of spacecraft and astronauts in space is 20 
of utmost importance for any space research and 21 
exploration mission. In addition to various biological 22 
and physiological challenges, fire hazard is a serious 23 
concern due to limited evacuation and fire 24 
suppression options. Furthermore, fire behavior is 25 
remarkably different in micro- and partial gravities 26 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>2 
 
</p>
<p>compared to normal gravity due to the reduction (or 1 
absence) of buoyancy [1–3]. There are situations 2 
where, for other conditions being the same, 3 
combustion occurs in reduced gravity but not in 4 
normal gravity. It has also been observed that steady 5 
flame spread can be achieved in microgravity in 6 
concurrent flows [2, 4], whereas on Earth, flame 7 
spread is usually regarded as an accelerating process 8 
for typical fuel bed sizes [5–7]. These fundamental 9 
differences in flame behavior have prompted many 10 
experimental studies to develop a better 11 
understanding of fire dynamics in reduced and 12 
microgravity conditions using drop towers [3, 8], 13 
parabolic flights [9–12], space shuttles [13], and 14 
facilities aboard the International Space Station [14–15 
16]. 16 
</p>
<p>For future missions such as NASA’s Artemis 17 
program, there are plans to send humans to the Moon 18 
for exploration and research. This program will 19 
include the establishment of a new space station in 20 
Lunar orbit (Gateway) and eventually the creation of 21 
a habitable Lunar base. These new missions bring a 22 
unique set of challenges due to the gravity level on the 23 
Moon, which is 1/6th of the Earth’s gravity (ge). It is 24 
well known that buoyancy plays a significant role in 25 
fire growth processes in normal gravity [5–7, 17, 18]. 26 
However, reduced buoyancy conditions in partial 27 
gravity can create more severe fire hazards compared 28 
to normal gravity [19–22]. The expected weaker 29 
buoyant flow in partial gravity can lead to smaller 30 
convective heat loss and a longer residence time of 31 
reactants in the flame stabilization zone. As a result, a 32 
material may be combustible over a wider range of 33 
oxygen concentrations and pressures in partial gravity 34 
than in normal gravity. Therefore, studying how a fire 35 
would behave in these partial gravity conditions 36 
becomes very important for the safe continuation of 37 
space exploration in such environments. 38 
</p>
<p>There have been only limited studies [1, 9–11, 21, 39 
22] on partial gravity flame behavior due to difficulty 40 
in performing experiments in partial gravity 41 
conditions (e.g., parabolic flights). Feier et al. [9] 42 
performed upward, and downward flame spread and 43 
extinction experiments over a thin solid in a partial 44 
gravity environment created in an airplane flying 45 
parabolic trajectories. A linearly increasing 46 
relationship with gravity was observed for flame 47 
spread rates and pyrolysis lengths with proportionality 48 
constants dependent on the pressure and sample 49 
width. They also developed a three-dimensional 50 
numerical model and obtained good agreement with 51 
the experiment. Similarly, Kleinhenz et al. 52 
[10]conducted parabolic flight experiments on 53 
upward flame spread over thin solid samples in 54 
different pressure and gravity conditions. Through 55 
pressure modeling, they successfully corelated the 56 
flame spread rates to pressures and gravity levels 57 
(with an empirical exponent for the pressure term, Vf 58 
~ p1.8g). In a recent study, Konno et al. [11] conducted 59 
parabolic flight experiments on downward flame 60 
spread over electric wire samples (i.e., metallic core 61 
</p>
<p>wrapped by low-density polyethylene insulation). In 62 
their results, the limiting oxygen concentrations and 63 
flame spread rates were deduced and found to have 64 
different dependencies on gravity levels for different 65 
oxygen concentrations, forced flow speeds, and wire 66 
characteristics (e.g., insulation thickness, core 67 
material). 68 
</p>
<p> Another way to generate artificial gravity is by 69 
using a centrifuge. This technique has been used in 70 
various previous studies [23–29] to generate hyper 71 
gravity by rotating the centrifuge in Earth’s normal 72 
gravity and allowing the sample fixture to swing in 73 
order to align it with the net acceleration vector. In 74 
order to generate artificial partial gravity levels, the 75 
centrifuge needs to be rotated in a microgravity 76 
facility (e.g., drop tower, sounding rocket). 77 
</p>
<p>One of the complications of using a centrifuge 78 
facility is the introduction of the Coriolis force [20, 79 
25, 29–31]. When hot combusting gas “rises” toward 80 
the axis of rotation, it experiences a Coriolis force 81 
orthogonal to the velocity vector, tilting the flow 82 
stream and hence the flame away from the radial line. 83 
In previous hyper gravity centrifuge studies, Coriolis 84 
force was assumed to cause negligible effects on the 85 
flame. For example, in the study of Durox et al. [25], 86 
for a 15 cm-long methane diffusion flame in a 5m-87 
arm-length centrifuge, the lateral deflection caused by 88 
the Coriolis effect was deduced to be less than 6 % of 89 
the flame length. In another study by Altenkirch et al. 90 
[24], errors caused by the Coriolis effects in flame 91 
length measurement were considered less than 6 % 92 
and was neglected. However, these effects have not 93 
been thoroughly evaluated for microgravity 94 
centrifuge. Furthermore, burning experiments using a 95 
centrifuge will need to be contained in a closed 96 
combustion chamber. If the chamber is not large 97 
enough, additional challenges, including induced flow 98 
due to recirculation in the limited space of the 99 
chamber, and oxygen depletion (especially for thick 100 
samples), are present. For a microgravity centrifuge, 101 
the experiment time duration is also limited by the 102 
hosting microgravity platform (e.g., ~5 s for drop 103 
tower, 20-30s for parabolic aircraft flights, and 1-5 104 
minutes for sounding rockets) and operations can be 105 
costly. Due to these difficulties in setting up an 106 
artificial partial gravity environment, there have been 107 
only a handful of experimental studies on flame 108 
dynamics in partial gravity. 109 
</p>
<p> These challenges can be addressed using 110 
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) techniques to 111 
understand experiment results and to guide future 112 
partial gravity tests. Some previous studies have 113 
developed numerical models for studying partial 114 
gravity flame spread in parabolic flights [32, 33]. 115 
However, there is lack of numerical studies that 116 
specifically investigated the effect of flame behavior 117 
in the partial gravity environment created using a 118 
rotating centrifuge, the objective of the present study. 119 
In this work, a numerical model is developed and 120 
validated against previous NASA microgravity 121 
centrifuge experiments [20]], and the effects of 122 </p>

<p>Coriolis force and flow recirculation are discussed. 1 
The developed model, with potential to be extended 2 
to different chamber geometry and fuel type, can 3 
serve as a tool to provide suggestions for the design of 4 
future centrifuge tests. It will also provide information 5 
on interpreting results from existing and future 6 
centrifuge burning tests. 7 
</p>

2. Numerical Model 9 
</p>
<p>A numerical model is developed using CFD 10 
software ANSYS FLUENT 20.1. Details of the 11 
numerical model can be found in [30, 31]. Below is a 12 
brief description of the model setup. 13 
</p>
<p>The geometry is based on previous experiments 14 
performed at the NASA Glenn Research Center using 15 
a microgravity centrifuge operated in a 5.2 s drop 16 
tower [20]. The experiments consisted of a simulated 17 
candle (i.e., a wick made of a porous, sintered bronze 18 
cylinder inserted into a small liquid fuel reservoir) 19 
mounted horizontally on a turntable (XZ plane) inside 20 
a dome-shaped chamber as shown in Fig. 1a. Heptane 21 
liquid fuel was replenished in the wick through 22 
capillarity as combustion took place. As the chamber 23 
fell in the drop tower, different artificial gravity levels 24 
were created by rotating the turntable at different 25 
angular velocities. Two cameras were fixed inside the 26 
chamber to record the flame growth process. 27 
</p>
<p> 28 
</p>

<p>in the simulations. 31

<p>In this numerical study, a CAD model is 33 
constructed based on the experimental setup, as 34 
shown in Fig. 1b. Dimensions of the model and wick 35 
are shown in Fig. 2. The wick is fixed on the rotating 36 
table such that its tip is at 32 cm from the axis of 37 
rotation (at x = -32 cm). Heptane fuel is assumed 38 
</p>
<p>coming out of the wick at a constant mass flow rate of 39 
2.083×10-6 kg/s (corresponding to a 100 W candle 40 
flame [34]). All chamber walls are defined with no-41 
slip (with respect to the rotating frame) and adiabatic 42 
conditions. 43 
</p>

<p>4 
 
</p>
<p>To generate the artificial gravity, the 1 
computational domain is rotated around the vertical 2 
(i.e., negative y-) axis. The rotational speeds are 3 
selected to match the tested conditions in the 4 
experiments: 0.003 ge, 0.012 ge, 0.075 ge, 0.2 ge, 0.3 5 
ge, 0.59 ge, and 1 ge (ge: earth gravity = 9.81 m/s2). 6 
While the wick is located at 32 cm from the axis of 7 
rotation, the rotational speeds are selected so that the 8 
desired artificial gravities are achieved at 30 cm since 9 
this location is approximately the center of the flame. 10 
</p>
<p>Conservation of mass, momentum, species and 11 
energy are solved using Finite Volume Method 12 
(FVM). Turbulence is modeled using the Reynolds-13 
averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations with a k-14 
omega SST model. For the gas-phase combustion, 15 
single step overall stoichiometric combustion is 16 
adopted: C7H16 + 11O2 → 8H2O + 7CO2. The reaction 17 
rate is assumed to be controlled by turbulent mixing 18 
(fast kinetic) and is calculated using the eddy 19 
dissipation model. 20 
</p>
<p>The initial conditions are set to 300K and zero 21 
relative velocity (with respect to the rotational 22 
chamber). This is to mimic the experimental setup. In 23 
the experiments, the chamber was spun up for a period 24 
of time before it was dropped (i.e., chamber gas was 25 
considered to rotate as a solid body). The initial gas 26 
composition is air. Small traces of combustion 27 
products (H2O and CO2) are included, as required by 28 
the eddy-dissipation reaction rate model in Fluent 29 
(initial mass fraction of the gas: 23.13% O2, 76.77% 30 
N2, 0.05% H2O, and 0.05% CO2). 31 
</p>
<p>Non-uniform meshing is used to optimize 32 
computational time and resources with finer meshes 33 
allocated near the wick where the flame is expected to 34 
grow. Finer meshes are also allocated near the walls 35 
to resolve the thermal and viscous boundary layers. A 36 
grid independence study is performed to ensure that 37 
the mesh resolution is appropriate [30]. Based on the 38 
results, final mesh selected consists of 8.14 million 39 
cells with the largest cell of volume 8.89 ×10-7 m3 and 40 
</p>
<p>the smallest 8.21 × 10-12 m3. A constant time step of 41 
0.01 s for a total of 500 timesteps is employed to 42 
capture 5 seconds of data aligning with the drop tower 43 
experimental duration. The simulations are performed 44 
on the Case High Performance Computing Cluster 45 
using 16 cores and 32 GB memory. The simulation 46 
time is ~ 16-24 hours for each case. 47 
</p>

3. Comparisons between Numerical and 49 
Experimental Results 50 
</p>
<p>3.1 Transient flame growth process 51 
</p>
<p>Figure 3 shows a side-by-side comparison of the 52 
flame growth process in the experiment [20] (left 53 
panes) and in the numerical simulation (right panes) 54 
for artificial gravity at 0.3 ge. A video version of this 55 
comparison can be found in the supplemental 56 
material. Numerical results on the right panes display 57 
the temperature contour with a 5% oxygen isoline 58 
(black line). The numerical simulation predicts a close 59 
resemblance of the flame shape observed in the 60 
experiment throughout the flame development 61 
process. After ignition, the flame grew “upwards” 62 
towards the center of rotation and tilted to the left due 63 
to the effect of Coriolis force in both the experiment 64 
and numerical simulation. 65 
</p>
<p>When the artificial gravity exceeds 0.075 ge, both 66 
experimental and numerical results exhibit the 67 
phenomenon of flame pulsation. This phenomenon 68 
has been observed for a wide range of fuel types, 69 
burner diameters, heat release rates, and Reynolds 70 
numbers [35-39]. Previous study suggested that it is 71 
caused by Kelvin-Helmholtz instability of the buoyant 72 
flow [39]. In this study, it is more obvious at a higher 73 
simulated ge (i.e., larger buoyant flow velocity). To 74 
illustrate this, Fig. 4 shows one cycle of the pulsation 75 
in the experiment (top row) and in the numerical 76 
simulation (bottom row) at 1ge. The corresponding 77 
video can be found in the supplemental material. 78 
</p>

<p>Figure 5 compares the flame profiles at different 8

</p>
<p>In this study, the blowing velocity on the wick 20 
surface (in the order of mm/s) is negligible compared 21 
to the buoyant flow. The Froude number ranges from 22 
~0.6 to ~0.002 and the flame is considered as 23 
buoyancy controlled in all simulated cases [40, 41]. In 24 
Reynolds–Froude scaling analysis, flame length is 25 
negatively correlated to the gravity level (e.g., ~𝑔𝑔−1/5 26 
by Delichatsios [40], ~𝑔𝑔−1/3 by Altenkirch et al. [42] 27 
and by D. Li et al. [41]). In the previous hyper gravity 28 
centrifuge experiments by Durox et al. [25], methane-29 
air jet diffusion flame length was observed to decrease 30 
with 𝑔𝑔−1/3 for gravity level greater than 2 ge, 31 
consistent with the Reynolds–Froude scaling analysis. 32 
However, between 1 ge to 2 ge, the flame length was 33 
observed to remain constant or even slightly increase 34 
with the gravity. They attributed this to interactions 35 
between vortices above the flame and the tip of the 36 
reaction zone. In the study of Sunderland et al. [28], 37 
flame lengths of laminar hydrogen, methane, ethane, 38 
</p>
<p>and propane flames from various experiments were 39 
comprehensively examined. The collected data 40 
covered a wide range of gravity level (1–15 ge). Their 41 
results showed that the Reynolds scaling (i.e., non-42 
dimensional flame length scales with Re) accurately 43 
correlated the flame length measurements at both 44 
Earth and elevated gravity, within experimental 45 
uncertainties. They concluded that the stoichiometric 46 
flame lengths at normal and elevated gravity are 47 
independent of the gravity level. This is also 48 
consistent with the observations for simulated partial 49 
gravity above 0.012 ge in this study. 50 
</p>

<p>4.3 Flow recirculation 39 
</p>
<p>Since the chamber is closed, buoyant flows can 40 
eventually recirculate back to the flame region and 41 
affect the flame behaviors. To explore this, 42 
simulations are extended to 7s (~ 2s longer than the 43 
original drop tower experiment duration). Figure 14 44 
</p>
<p>compares the flame growth and dynamics of the flow 45 
for the 1 ge case at 1s and 7s. In this study, the 46 
centrifuge is rotating clockwise (viewed from the top), 47 
the Coriolis force is always to the left with respect to 48 
the flow direction. This results in a counterclockwise 49 
circulation of the flow (see the white streamlines in 50 
Fig. 14). 51 
</p>

underestimation of the flame angles (Angles B and C) 9

</p>
<p>Notably, this process of flame tilting in the 11 
opposite direction was not observed in the 12 
experiments. It is because the recirculation effect only 13 
becomes prominent in the simulation after ~ 5s (7 s 14 
for 1ge and longer for lower artificial gravity cases) 15 
while the experiments durations were limited to 5.2 s. 16 
</p>
<p>Another factor that has likely reduced or 17 
eliminated the recirculating flow in the experiments is 18 
the presence of equipment such as cameras, sensors, 19 
cables, and other materials within the chamber. These 20 
components act as obstructions disturbing the 21 
recirculation flow and preventing the recirculating 22 
flow from pushing the flame to the opposite direction. 23 
</p>
<p>It is important to note that the simulation results 24 
presented in this study assume that the chamber is 25 
empty and does not consist of any obstructions, except 26 
the liquid wick. In additional simulations with random 27 
obstructions inside the chamber [30], flow 28 
recirculation is not observed even at longer durations. 29

<p> 30 
5. Conclusions and Future Works 31 
</p>
<p>A numerical model is developed to investigate the 32 
dynamics of flames in simulated partial gravity 33 
environments in a microgravity centrifuge. The 34 
objective is to develop fundamental understanding of 35 
how the Coriolis force affects the flame 36 
characteristics. Various artificial partial gravity levels 37 
ranging from 0.003 ge to 1 ge are achieved by 38 
adjusting the centrifuge's rotational speed. 39 
Simulations are compared with previous NASA’s 40 
experiments and the effects of Coriolis force and flow 41 
</p>
<p>recirculation are presented. Key findings are as 42 
follows. 43 
</p>
<p>The model is shown to be able to capture the 44 
overall fire behavior observed in the experiments. 45 
This includes the flame shapes and flame tilt angles at 46 
different artificial gravity levels. Flame pulsation and 47 
bending behaviors at larger artificial gravities are also 48 
captured. However, the pulsation frequency is 49 
underestimated. Further tuning of the model is 50 
warranted to further investigation on the pulsation 51 
phenomenon. 52 
</p>
<p>In both experimental and numerical results, as the 53 
artificial gravity increases from 0.003 ge to 0.012 ge, 54 
flame transits from spherical to elongated shapes. At 55 
0.075 ge, the flame starts exhibiting lateral tilting due 56 
to Coriolis effect, and at and above 0.59 ge, the flame 57 
exhibits two-angle bending phenomenon. Detailed 58 
examinations of local Coriolis and buoyancy forces 59 
provided insights into the transition of flame shapes. 60 
The Coriolis force tilts the flame in the tangential 61 
direction while the buoyancy force is responsible for 62 
keeping the flame vertical. The Coriolis acceleration 63 
and the buoyancy forces are both positively corelated 64 
to the artificial gravity level. As a result, the flame tilt 65 
angle remains similar in all simulated artificial 66 
gravities. 67 
</p>
<p>The variations in temperature, density, velocity, 68 
and various forces along the flame length are 69 
examined in details. For all simulated artificial 70 
gravities, the buoyancy force, directly linked to the 71 
gas temperature, initially rises, reaches a maximum in 72 
the flame region, and subsequently diminishes. 73 
Conversely, the Coriolis force, influenced by flow 74 
momentum exhibits different trends in lower and 75 
higher artificial gravities. At low ge, the Coriolis force 76 
remains roughly constant along the flame length. For 77 
artificial gravity at 0.59 ge and higher, the Coriolis 78 
force remains similar in the bottom portion of the 79 
flame but increases in the top portion of the flame due 80 
to the increase in the local density and comparatively 81 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>13 
 
</p>
<p>lesser decrease in relative velocity. This increase in 1 
the Coriolis force contributes to the more pronounced 2 
tilt of the flame and explains the flame bending 3 
phenomenon observed in the experiments at the 4 
corresponding artificial gravity levels. 5 
</p>
<p>Potential impact of flow recirculation within the 6 
combustion chamber's confined volume are also 7 
analyzed. It is observed that flow recirculation in the 8 
limited volume of the combustion chamber affects the 9 
flame behavior. For artificial 1ge simulated case, the 10 
flame is tilted to the left in the direction of Coriolis 11 
force for the first five seconds, after which the 12 
recirculating flow overcomes the Coriolis 13 
acceleration and pushes the flame back to its neutral 14 
position (i.e., being straight). However, this was not 15 
observed in the experiments because of the short 16 
duration and equipment components disturbing the 17 
recirculation flow. Nevertheless, it is imperative not 18 
to overlook these factors when assessing the overall 19 
dynamics of the system. 20 
</p>
<p>To conclude, the developed numerical model can 21 
be used for the examination of flame dynamics within 22 
a simulated partial gravity environment, thereby 23 
providing important inputs for designing future 24 
centrifuge partial gravity experiments. However, it 25 
should be noted that the observations in this numerical 26 
study are based on the specific setup (e.g., chamber 27 
size, fuel source arrangement and location) in the 28 
previous NASA experiments. To extend the model to 29 
different chamber and fuel arrangement, 30 
modifications of initial and boundary conditions will 31 
be required. It should also be noted that the current 32 
model does not include radiation and soot formation, 33 
and it assumes a constant mass flow rate of the fuel 34 
source, overlooking heat feedback from the flame and 35 
simplifying experimental conditions. Although it 36 
provides a good estimate of overall fire dynamics, 37 
incorporating flame and soot formation, and a user-38 
defined function for simulating fuel evaporation rate 39 
in the future work will offer further insights into the 40 
impact of artificial gravity on the detailed flame 41 
structure. This consideration will be part of our future 42 
studies. 43
