<p>reduced gravity aircraft has shown that there can be enhanced flammability (flames can exist 
at lower ambient oxygen concentrations compared to terrestrial gravity) at partial gravity 
levels close to those found on the moon. This was identified as a risk for the Human Landing 
System program. However, lunar gravity testing is largely limited to using a centrifuge in the 
NASA GRC 5.2 second drop tower. This paper discusses the fundamentals of material 
flammability and extinction, and develops a Damköhler-number based estimate of the blow-
off flammability limit. This relationship is compared to the results from material flammability 
tests in a highspeed blow-off facility at the NASA White Sands Test Facility (WSTF) (1-g) and 
the Glenn Research Center (GRC) Zero Gravity Facility partial gravity centrifuge. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
ASA has focused on material selection as a primary means of fire prevention. The method for selecting fire safe 
materials has been NASA STD 6001 Test 1 (hereafter referred to as Test 1)1. Test 1 is an upward flammability 
</p>
<p>test that uses a consistent ignition source (known as the chemical igniter) that is considered to be a very strong ignition 
source1. If the flame damaged zone is found to be less than 15.2 cm (6”), the material is determined to be self-
extinguishing for the test conditions. The concept was that with a strong ignition source in upward spread, the test will 
be conservative. The purpose of the test is not to identify materials that will never burn but rather to identify materials 
that do not burn easily and will self-extinguish in most situations. Given the test design, for most materials, Test 1 is 
an ignition test, if the material has enough specific heat or low enough heat release, it will pass but it might not pass 
with a bigger igniter or if the material were 
slightly preheated. In the case of thin materials 
of limited spread rate, it is possible for Test 1 to 
function as a flammability test.

<p>II. Flammability Limits 
The existence of a low-speed quenching extinguishment boundary and a higher speed blowoff boundary was 
</p>
<p>predicted numerically for solid fuels in stagnation flow5. This phenomenon was subsequently shown experimentally6 
for thin cellulosic materials in opposed flow (Fig 1). Fire and flames can be divided into several classes: gaseous 
(premixed and nonpremixed (diffusion)), with diffusion flames including gaseous flames and liquid and solid fuels 
(charring, noncharring and surface burning), and smoldering combustion of solid fuels. This paper will discuss solid 
fuel combustion with some overlap with surface smoldering/char combustion as schematically depicted in figure2. 
With the exception of smoldering combustion and burning of metals and carbon, all materials burn in the gas phase. 
This fuel phase-change creates a fundamental difference between condensed fuel combustion and that of gaseous 
fuels. Continued burning of the fuel requires sufficient energy feedback from the flame to the fuel to pyrolyze or 
vaporize more fuel. This feedback controls the flame size and spread rate. Extinction of the gaseous flame also occurs 
by different processes than extinguishment of the smoldering char phase. Many of the nonmelting compounds that 
must pyrolyze to burn, do not completely convert to gaseous compounds and instead also produce a substantial mass 
fraction of char which is primarily carbon and burns in the solid phase. This class of fuels are known as charring fuels 
of which the most common example is cellulosic materials (wood, cotton, paper). The glowing coals are the surface 
combustion of the char residue from the pyrolysis. Many important spacecraft materials (Nomex and Kevlar) are also 
charring materials. This work will focus on extinguishment of the gas phase flame over the solid fuel rather than the 
surface reaction of the charring solid. 
</p>
<p>Flame spread over solid fuels is often modeled as a progressive ignition process where the flame heats the next 
portion of fuel to produce the necessary fuel vapor and then provides the ignition pilot, enabling the flame to spread 
forward7,8. Figure 28 depicts the most important transport processes in an upward spreading flame. The flame base at 
the bottom end of the flame is the attachment point of the flame 
where oxygen diffuses to the flame surface from the free stream and 
reacts with the fuel vapor emerging from the fuel surface by surface 
blowing and diffusion. If the incoming oxidizer flow is too fast for 
the flame chemistry, the flame will be blown downstream (blowoff). 
</p>
<p>Beneath the flame is a region designated as the pyrolysis length. 
This region of fuel has been heated to the pyrolysis temperature and 
is emitting the unburned fuel vapor that supports the flame. Further 
downstream is the heating length portion of the fuel which is being 
heated from ambient temperature to the pyrolysis temperature by the 
flame. The major transport mechanisms for a thin fuel are 
convective/conductive heating of the fuel by the flame, radiative 
heating of the fuel by the flame, and radiative heat loss from the fuel. 
As the fuel becomes thicker, conductive transport in the solid phase 
fuel becomes more significant although for upward buoyant spread, 
heat transfer from the gas phase has been shown to be remain more 
significant than conduction in the solid phase9. The process is similar 
for opposed (downward) flame spread except the view factor for 
radiation from the flame to the fuel surface (Qfr*) becomes less 
significant and the preheat length is shorter. 
</p>
<p>Under normal gravity conditions, concurrent flame spread is faster than opposed flow flame spread and often 
grows continuously until the fire becomes quite large. Opposed spread can achieve steady conditions in normal 
gravity. The Saffire experiment found that both concurrent and opposed spread could be steady for samples roughly 
0.8 m in length for a thin cellulosic fuel10. The progressive or creeping ignition process has been represented as the 
ratio of the heated length and the time to heat this length of fuel to the pyrolysis temperature and to ignite the gas.7,8

Both works successfully address the problem with similar concepts, basing the flame extinction problem in terms of 
heat transfer and chemical kinetics processes.

<p>Experimental Method 
The goal was to measure the change in the extinction limit between 1-g testing and lunar-g testing in the centrifuge. 
</p>
<p>NASA Test 1 has a 15 cm (6 inch) burn criterion, so the test time is much longer than the 5 seconds available in the 
drop tower. Consequently a 5 second extinction criterion test program was developed to determine the change in the 
extinction limit in 1-g and lunar-g and apply this offset to NASA Test 1 flammability limits for lunar material 
certification. The approach used in this test program was to conduct a study of concurrent-flow flammability-limits in

<p>the planned NASA lunar habitat pressure of 56.5 kPa (8.2 psia) for several materials where the higher speed blow-off

program that included work at the NASA White Sands Test Facility (WSTF) for the 1-g buoyant flow testing and the 
NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC) Zero-Gravity Research Facility (ZGRF) for a quiescent lunar gravity 
environment. Major challenges of this comparison were the inherent difference in the ignition due to the orientation 
as will be described below and the short test time provided by the ZGF (5.2 seconds). This 5.2 second limitation 
required the testing to be based on a 5 second extinction limit rather than the 15 cm (6 inch) burn limit in NASA Test 
1. Since the testing was targeting a flammability limit rather than an ignition limit, the materials were ignited in a 
known flammable condition and the environment was changed to the test condition by reducing the oxygen 
concentration (ZGRF) or increasing the gas velocity (WSTF).

<p>A. NASA White Sands Test Facility (WSTF) Upward High-flow Oxygen Index Testing 
The WSTF testing was conducted using a WSTF 1.4 m3 (50 ft3) 
</p>
<p>chamber that could provide continuous operation at 57 kPa. Installed 
in the chamber was a 7.4 cm ID by 30 cm long quartz tube duct (Fig 
5) where the 5.2 cm by 14.0 cm samples were tested. This system was 
based on the HOI ISO Standard.18 Below the test section was a flow 
conditioning section above an inlet plenum. The flow was supplied 
by a mass flow system which established the desired oxygen 
concentration and flow rate. The sample material was sandwiched 
between two, u-shaped, thin stainless-steel plates with the open end 
oriented downwards towards the igniter. The igniter was a retractable 
2.2 mm OD ceramic rod that swung away from the sample after 
ignition. The ceramic rod was heated by 0.29 mm Kanthal-A wire 
woven through the 4 holes in the ceramic. The front and the back of 
the sample were recorded by video cameras. After installation in the 
flow duct, and pressure was established in the chamber, the sample 
was exposed to a 10 cm/s primer flow at the target oxygen 
concentration. The igniter was activated until the sample was ignited. 
The igniter was retracted, and the flame was allowed to establish for 
1 second before the flow was increased to the test condition at the 
same oxygen concentration. The flow increase occurs in 
approximately a second. If the flame extinguished in 5 seconds, it was

<p>B. NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC) Partial Gravity Testing 
The GRC testing was conducted using the centrifuge test facility in the ZGRF 5.2 second drop tower (Fig. 6).3 The 
</p>
<p>chamber is 299 l in volume with an I.D. of 81.2 cm and an internal height if 30.5 cm. To achieve lunar gravity, the 
chamber was rotated at 20.4 RPM for several minutes before the drop (I.e. onset of microgravity) to bring the air in 
the chamber to near solid body rotation. In this configuration, (during the drop) the gravity vector was radially 
outwards, and the resulting buoyant flow was radially inward To achieve upward spread the long axis of the sample 
is oriented radially and ignited on the outside edge so the resulting buoyant flow is concurrent with the direction of 
flame spread. Owing to the short radius of rotation, there was a significant gravity gradient. The flames, however, 
(especially when near the extinction limits) did not spread significantly during the drop, so the g-variation was 
considered to have a minimal impact on the results. The short radius of rotation also caused a significant Coriolis force 
in the tangential direction19. Initial tests found that the effect was mitigated by orienting the sample surface parallel to 
the axis of rotation (long edge down) so the Coriolis flow was impeded by the sample surface. The igniter was a 
ceramic igniter very similar to the one used at WSTF except it withdrew vertically. To increase the number of tests 
for repeatability, two samples were ignited and tested in each drop. The samples were several cm apart and offset 
vertically (Fig. 6) so they did not interfere with each other. The ignition region of the sample was exposed to a slow 
flow (~30 cm/s) of gas with an oxygen concentration 5 to 10% higher than the ambient. This enabled sample ignition 
in a chamber condition where the sample might be near the flammability limit. During the ignition interval, the drop 
rig is hanging at the top of the tower and is spinning to produce a 1/6 g radial acceleration in addition to the 1-g vertical 
force. This resulting buoyant flow primarily directed laterally across the sample is less successful at heating the 
unburned sample than the purely upward flow at WSTF. The drop tower operations require the system to operate 
autonomously so the entire process was controlled automatically by the drop rig. The ignition duration and the oxygen 
enhancement were varied based on experience with ignitability of the material being tested. After a prescribed 
duration, the igniter is turned off and retracted. One second later, the oxygen enhanced flow is turned off and the

<p>A. Compensation Factor Determination 
The method to determine the compensation factor involved performing a best fit line to the WSTF limit points 
</p>
<p>(black circles). The best fit line to those points is the estimated dependence of the limiting oxygen concentration on 
forced flow velocity. To get a conservative estimate of the slope (and thus the compensation factor) we used the one-
tailed 95 percent confidence interval. Table 2 shows both the best fit line and the conservative estimate from the 95 
percent confidence interval. To estimate the change in the flammability limit at 1-g compared to lunar-g, the 
extrapolated value from the WSTF testing can be compared to the extrapolated value at lunar g. A major limitation to 
this analysis is identification of the relevant buoyant velocity which is an area worthy of significant research. In a 
forced-flow low-gravity experiment, the average duct velocity can be known exactly, however, due to effects such as 
boundary layer growth and thermal expansion, the velocity varies with time and location so while the average duct 
velocity is representative of the flow field, it is not necessarily the same value as the velocity at the controlling location. 
For buoyant flames, with or without forced flow, the velocity at any location varies similarly to the non-buoyant case 
but it is not easily measured, particularly because the location of greatest interest is not known. For flames on the CKL 
side, the velocity at the flame tip can be assumed to be likely the most important location but this has not been verified 
or measured accurately. In a buoyant boundary layer, the velocity at the flame tip would not be comparable to the 
average duct velocity in non-buoyant cases. The 1-g buoyant velocity was estimated by Takahashi16 to be 35 cm/s, 
but the lunar gravity velocity is not known. The lunar gravity velocity has been estimated20, for spreading flames, by 
equating the buoyant boundary layer thickness with the forced flow boundary layer. However, both of these estimates 
are difficult to verify. Furthermore, near the extinction limit, the flame is weaker resulting in less buoyant flow. 
Consequently, given the absence of a common understanding of the relevant velocity, the buoyant velocity is 
considered to be not determined in this paper. Given this uncertainty, 35 cm/s could be considered to be a 
conservatively high estimate of the buoyant 1-g velocity. Since the lunar velocity is also not known, extrapolating to 
zero flow would give a conservative estimate of the limit. Extrapolating the worst-case 95% confidence interval slope 
from the data for each material (Table 2) from the presumed 1-g buoyant velocity of 35 cm/s to zero would give a 
conservative 1-g to lunar-g flammability delta that could be applied to other test methods. These values are tabulated 
in Table 2. The worst-case delta for these materials can be seen to be 2.3%. 
</p>
<p>The Beefy-T smoldering data are included because the NASA emphasis is on complete extinction, however since 
smoldering is a surface reaction rather than a gas-phase flame, the Damköhler number blow-off limit would not be 
expected to be applicable. However, it is clear in figure 10 that the extinction shows similar flow dependence. 
</p>

<p>Figures 11 and 12 present the extinction data for polycarbonate and Nomex Velcro. For both of these materials, 
the lunar-g buoyant extinction is at substantially higher oxygen concentrations that the WSTF data. One possible 
explanation for this is that the quench boundary for these materials shift to higher velocities than seen in the lunar-g 
tests, putting the lunar-gravity tests on the quenching (HTL) branch. This shift of the HTL could be attributed to 
increased radiative loss (Eq. 8) resulting from higher pyrolysis temperatures. This mechanism was suggested by 
Takahashi16 who saw similar behavior for several high temperature materials in downward spread. This mechanism 
merits additional study as it suggests that high temperature materials may not exhibit enhanced flammability at lunar-
g. This would be significant because most of the candidate materials for enhanced oxygen use are higher pyrolysis 
temperature materials. In addition, this behavior requires additional study because there may still be a flammability 
enhancement at Martian-g levels since Martian buoyant flows may still be on the blow-off (CKL) branch. 
</p>
<p>IV. Conclusion 
Comparative testing in 1-g under forced flow and buoyant flow at lunar-g conditions has demonstrated that for 
</p>
<p>some materials, there is a clear increased flammability at lunar gravity conditions. Other, higher temperature materials 
may show decreased flammability at lunar conditions than would be predicted from 1-g testing. This suggests that 
increased flammability at lunar conditions may not be a universal issue, however further research is needed to validate 
this possibility. At Martian g-levels, the buoyant flow will be higher and increased flammability is possible for 
materials that do not exhibit it at lunar-g levels. 
</p>
